# Lesson 10: Derivatives for Retail Investors

*Why derivatives tempt individuals, how the UK, the US and India each sell them, what the regulators' data says about outcomes, the structural reasons the odds tilt against individuals, leveraged funds and crypto perpetuals, and five questions to ask before any trade.*

---

## Nine in ten

In August 2026, India's securities regulator published its latest study of individuals trading futures and options. Across the year to March 2026, **87.7%** of individual traders lost money. That was the good news: a year earlier the figure had been nearly **91%**, and over the three years to March 2024 it had been **93%**. Even after two years of deliberate cooling, individual traders in SEBI's sample lost about **₹91,685 crore**, roughly $10 billion, in a single year.

The striking thing about the data is not India. It is how consistent the picture is everywhere regulators have looked. In the UK, firms selling contracts for difference must publish the share of their retail accounts that lose money, and the figures run from about half to about three-quarters. In the US, where the most popular retail product is now an option that expires the same day it is bought, no single official figure exists, but the academic studies point the same way. Individuals now trade derivatives on a scale unthinkable a decade ago, and most of them lose.

This lesson explains why, market by market, without moralising. The products are not evil and some people use them well. But the structure of the game is knowable, and knowing it is the best protection there is.

---

## Why derivatives tempt individuals

- **Leverage.** A small stake controls a large position, so a good day feels life-changing.
- **Lottery-like payoffs.** A cheap out-of-the-money option feels like a ticket with a small cost and a huge prize.
- **Speed.** Same-day expiries and slick apps give instant feedback, which feels more like a game than an investment.
- **Hidden costs.** Spreads, overnight financing, time decay and taxes are each small, but they compound against anyone who trades often.

Each of the first three is a feature that professionals use deliberately and price carefully. The fourth is the one that turns them against individuals.

## The UK: contracts for difference and spread bets

A **contract for difference (CFD)** is a cash-settled contract with a trading firm on the price change of an asset. Buy a CFD on £10,000 of a company's shares and you gain or lose exactly as if you owned them, without owning them, paying only a deposit and a daily financing charge. A **spread bet** has the same economics but is treated as gambling for tax, so gains are free of capital gains tax for UK individuals. Both are cash-settled forwards with a broker, rolled over each night (Lesson 2). London-listed IG Group, CMC Markets and Plus500 are among the world's largest providers.

Since August 2019, the Financial Conduct Authority has limited how much leverage a retail client can use.

On individual shares the limit is **5 to 1**: a £10,000 position needs at least £2,000 of margin. Firms must close a client's positions when their funds fall to **half** the required margin, retail clients **cannot lose more than the money in their account** (negative balance protection), and trading bonuses are banned. The FCA went further with two outright bans for retail customers: **binary options** in 2019 and **crypto derivatives** in January 2021. When it reopened retail access to crypto exchange-traded notes in October 2025, the ban on crypto derivatives stayed in place.

Every CFD firm must also tell clients what share of its retail accounts lose money. The published figures typically range from about **half to three-quarters**; several of the largest providers report around two-thirds or more. How do the firms themselves make money? Partly from the **spread** between their buying and selling prices and the **overnight financing** charged on every open position. And partly, for many firms, from **taking the other side** of clients' trades rather than passing them to the market, so that client losses are, in part, the firm's revenue. That is legal and disclosed, and it is worth knowing.

## The US: options on your phone, and the zero-day boom

Commission-free trading apps opened listed options to millions of Americans. "Free" is a pricing choice, not a gift: most US retail brokers are paid by the market makers who execute their customers' orders, a practice called **payment for order flow**. The UK's regulator has long regarded it as incompatible with its rules, and the EU's ban took full effect in mid-2026; in the US it remains legal and is how much of the industry earns its living.

The volumes are extraordinary. OCC, the industry's clearing house, cleared **15.26 billion** contracts in 2025, up **24%**, a sixth consecutive annual record. The fastest-growing corner is **zero-days-to-expiry (0DTE)** options, which expire on the day they are traded. Cboe began listing S&P 500 options for every weekday in 2022, and the share of S&P 500 index options volume in same-day contracts has grown from about **5%** in 2016 to **43%** in 2023, and to a record **66.2%** in July 2026. Retail traders account for roughly half of it.

Lesson 5 showed why 0DTE options behave so violently: two days from expiry, gamma near the strike becomes a spike, and on expiry day itself an option can swing from worthless to valuable and back within minutes. That is what makes them thrilling, and what worries regulators. If dealers who sell these options must hedge large, fast-changing deltas, their flows could amplify intraday moves (Lesson 9); researchers and regulators are still debating how much they do in practice.

## India: from the world's busiest options market to a crackdown

Cheap weekly options on the Nifty and Bank Nifty, small contract sizes, discount brokers with slick apps, and a wave of new investors after the pandemic made India the world's largest options market by number of contracts. Most of the activity was retail money buying cheap, short-dated options, often on expiry day itself.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) studied the outcomes and did not like them. Its 2024 study found that **93%** of more than ten million individual traders had lost money over the three years to March 2024, with aggregate losses above **₹1.8 lakh crore**. So from November 2024 it tightened the rules in phases: much larger minimum contract sizes, only one weekly expiry per exchange, option premiums collected upfront, extra margin on expiry day, and intraday monitoring of position limits; then, through 2025, open interest measured in delta terms, tighter position limits and a pre-open auction for futures. The government added a higher securities transaction tax from April 2026 (Lesson 11 has the details).

Volumes collapsed, dragging global exchange-traded volume down 42% in 2025 (Lesson 1). Fewer people traded and total losses fell. But the base rate barely moved: **87.7%** still lost money, and the average loss per trader edged up slightly, to about **₹1.17 lakh**. SEBI's study adds some telling detail. Options accounted for about **92%** of individuals' losses. About **59%** of index options turnover happened on the expiry day itself. Transaction costs alone came to about **₹25,000 crore** in the year, of which the transaction tax was about a quarter. And of the traders who had lost money two years running and kept trading, about nine in ten lost again.

## Why individuals tend to lose

The pattern is structural, not bad luck, and it has four parts.

1. **Somebody earns the spread.** Professional market makers earn the gap between buying and selling prices on every trade. A trader who turns over their account many times a year pays it many times.
2. **Option buyers pay the insurance premium.** Lesson 4 showed that implied volatility usually exceeds realised volatility. Retail flow mostly *buys* options, and especially short-dated, out-of-the-money ones that are expensive relative to their chance of paying out.
3. **Leverage forces losing positions closed before any recovery.** Lesson 6's margin account is a retail story too: the position is closed at the bottom, so the loss becomes permanent even if the market recovers.
4. **Costs compound.** Spreads, commissions, financing, time decay and taxes are each small; multiplied by hundreds of trades they become decisive.

None of this means an individual cannot use derivatives sensibly. Buying a put to protect a long-held portfolio, or selling covered calls on shares you are happy to sell, are ordinary uses of the tools in Lesson 5. What the data says is that **frequent, leveraged, short-dated speculation** is, for most people, a reliable way to transfer money to professionals.

## Two traps that look like simple products

### Leveraged and inverse funds

A leveraged exchange-traded fund promises twice, or three times, the **daily** move of an index; an inverse fund promises the opposite of it. They use futures and swaps to do it, and reset their exposure every evening. That one word, "daily", changes everything over longer periods.

In a choppy market where the index rises 5% and falls back to where it started, again and again, the index ends flat but a twice-leveraged fund loses money, and a twice-inverse fund loses more. After every fall, the fund resets to a smaller base, so the next rise does not fully repair the damage. In a steady trend the effect runs the other way, and the leveraged fund gains more than twice the index. These funds are tools for a day or two, not for a portfolio; their prospectuses say so, and many holders do not read them.

### Crypto perpetual futures

The most traded crypto derivative is the **perpetual future**, a future that never expires. Instead of converging at expiry, it is held close to the spot price by a **funding payment** every few hours: when the perpetual trades above spot, buyers pay sellers; when it trades below, sellers pay buyers.

Offshore exchanges allow leverage of 100 times or more, at which a 1% move against you wipes out your margin and the exchange's systems close the position automatically. When prices move sharply, those automatic liquidations become forced selling that triggers more liquidations, a margin spiral running at machine speed, around the clock. On **10 October 2025** more than **$19 billion** of leveraged crypto positions were liquidated in about a day, the largest such event on record (Lesson 13). In the US, CFTC-regulated "perpetual-style" futures with leverage of up to 10 times launched in July 2025; in the UK, crypto derivatives remain off limits to retail clients.

## Questions to ask before any retail derivative trade

> 1. **What is the most I can lose, including a margin call or an overnight gap?**
> 2. **Who is on the other side, and how does the provider make money from me?**
> 3. **What are all the costs: spread, commission, financing, time decay and tax?**
> 4. **If I am right about the direction but wrong about the timing, what happens?**
> 5. **Would I still take this position without the leverage?**

## Check yourself

**1. You open a £10,000 CFD position on a share at the UK maximum of 5 to 1. How much margin must you post? The share falls 10%. What happens?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Margin = £10,000 ÷ 5 = **£2,000**. A 10% fall loses **£1,000**, half your margin. That is exactly the FCA's 50% close-out level, so the firm will close the position (unless you add money), turning a 10% move in the share into a **50% loss** of your stake.

</details>

**2. An index rises 10% one day and falls 10% the next. Where does the index end, and where does a fund that delivers twice the daily move end?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Index: 100 × 1.10 × 0.90 = **99**, down 1%. Twice-leveraged fund: 100 × 1.20 × 0.80 = **96**, down 4%: four times the index's loss, not two. Volatility drags leveraged funds down even when the market goes nowhere.

</details>

**3. In SEBI's sample, 78.6 lakh individual traders lost ₹91,685 crore net in the year to March 2026. What was the average net loss per trader?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

₹91,685 crore ÷ 78.6 lakh = ₹916,850 million ÷ 7.86 million ≈ **₹1.17 lakh** each (about $1,300). Note the base: across all brokers there were about 87.5 lakh active individual traders, so dividing by that larger number would understate the average in SEBI's sample.

</details>

**4. A perpetual future trades 0.03% above spot at a funding time, and the funding rate is set equal to that gap. You are long £50,000 of exposure. What do you pay or receive?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

When the perpetual is above spot, longs pay shorts: 0.03% × £50,000 = **£15** for that funding period. Three times a day, that is £45 a day, or more than £16,000 over a year on a £50,000 position: one of the hidden costs of holding a leveraged crypto position when the crowd is long.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 11

- Regulators' data is consistent: **most individual derivatives traders lose money**: 87.7% in India in FY2025-26; about half to three-quarters of UK CFD accounts.
- The UK sells **CFDs and spread bets**, with leverage capped (5 to 1 on shares), margin close-out at 50%, and losses capped at the account balance.
- The US sells **listed options on apps**, paid for by **payment for order flow**; **0DTE** options reached 66.2% of S&P 500 options volume in July 2026.
- India **cooled the world's busiest options market** through product design and taxes; participation and losses fell, the odds barely changed.
- Individuals lose for **structural** reasons: spreads, the volatility premium, forced close-outs and compounding costs.
- **Leveraged funds** deliver a multiple of the day, not the year; **crypto perpetuals** pair 100x leverage with automatic liquidation.

Lesson 11 widens the lens from retail traders to the whole market in each of the three countries: regulators, venues, strengths and the live issues of 2026.

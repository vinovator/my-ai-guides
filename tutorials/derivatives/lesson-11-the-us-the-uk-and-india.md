# Lesson 11: The US, the UK and India

*Three very different derivatives markets: who regulates them, where they trade and clear, what each does best, and the live issues of 2026.*

---

## Three markets, three personalities

The machinery of Lessons 2 to 8 is the same everywhere: forwards, futures, options and swaps, cleared through central counterparties and backed by margin. But each country has bolted it together differently, and the differences explain most of what you read in the news.

The **United States** leads in exchange-traded futures and options: the deepest interest rate futures in the world, the benchmark oil and grain contracts, and a retail options culture with no parallel. The **United Kingdom** leads in over-the-counter swaps and currencies: half of all OTC interest rate derivatives trading is booked in London, and its clearing houses clear almost all the world's swaps. **India** leads in the sheer number of retail options contracts, and has spent two years deliberately cooling that market down.

This lesson takes each in turn and ends with them side by side.

The **Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC)**, created in 1974, oversees futures, options on futures and most swaps: interest rate, currency, commodity and broad index credit swaps. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees options on shares and stock indices, and security-based swaps such as credit default swaps on a single company. The split is an accident of history. Futures grew out of farm markets regulated under agricultural law, while options on shares grew out of securities law. Firms often deal with both regulators for closely related products, and proposals to merge the two have come and gone for decades.

After 2008, the **Dodd-Frank Act** of 2010 rebuilt the swaps market. Standard swaps must now be cleared, many must trade on regulated electronic platforms called swap execution facilities, every trade is reported to a swap data repository, and dealers must register and post margin on uncleared trades.

### The venues

- **CME Group**, in Chicago and New York, owns the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, the Chicago Board of Trade, NYMEX and COMEX. It set a record in 2025, averaging **28.1 million** contracts a day, up 6%.
- **Cboe** is home to S&P 500 index options and the VIX, and to the same-day options boom of Lesson 10.
- **ICE** runs futures exchanges in the US and owns the New York Stock Exchange.
- **OCC**, the Options Clearing Corporation, clears every listed US equity option, whichever of the many options exchanges it trades on.

### Signature features

**The interest rate engine.** US Treasury futures and options averaged **8.3 million** contracts a day at CME in 2025, and SOFR futures and options another **5.4 million**. SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, replaced dollar LIBOR as the benchmark for floating payments. The hedge-fund basis trade of Lesson 6 links this market directly to the bond market.

**Mandatory Treasury clearing.** Central clearing of eligible US Treasury cash trades is due by **31 December 2026**, with repo following by **30 June 2027**. It is one of the biggest changes to the market's plumbing in decades, pushing the government bond market towards the clearing model that derivatives adopted after 2008.

**Global commodity benchmarks.** WTI crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas trade on NYMEX, and corn, soybeans and wheat on the Chicago Board of Trade, setting prices for producers worldwide. The [commodity guide](?slug=commodity-markets&lesson=lesson-09-the-us-the-uk-and-india) covers them in depth.

**An options culture.** Commission-free apps, meme stocks and same-day options have made listed options a mass-market product in the US, unlike in the UK (Lesson 10).

**Prediction markets.** Event contracts pay a fixed amount if something happens, from a Federal Reserve decision to a football result: binary options on the real world. Kalshi, an exchange regulated by the CFTC, argues that all its event contracts, including sports, are swaps under the CFTC's exclusive federal jurisdiction. Several states say sports contracts are gambling that they should regulate. The courts have split. In April 2026 a divided Third Circuit sided with Kalshi against New Jersey; in September 2026 the Sixth Circuit ruled unanimously the other way, finding that sports contracts are not swaps and that federal law does not override Ohio's and Tennessee's gambling laws. With the appeals courts divided, the question looks headed for the Supreme Court. The sums are already large: in July 2026 alone, boosted by the football World Cup, Kalshi handled about **$37.7 billion** of trading, and together with Polymarket the total passed **$50 billion**.

### What to watch

- The rollout of Treasury clearing from December 2026, and whether it strains or strengthens the bond market.
- How the prediction-market split is resolved, by the courts or by Congress.
- Whether same-day options keep growing, and whether they spread from indices to single shares.

## The United Kingdom: the world's swap shop

London is the world's hub for over-the-counter derivatives. In April 2025, **half** of all OTC interest rate derivatives trading worldwide was booked by UK sales desks, against **24%** in the US, according to the BIS Triennial Survey. London also handled **38%** of global currency trading.

### Why London?

- **Time zone.** A London trading day overlaps with both Asia's afternoon and New York's morning.
- **English law.** Most international derivatives contracts are written under English or New York law, and many ISDA agreements choose English law.
- **History and people.** The Eurodollar market grew up in London in the 1960s, and the dealers, lawyers and infrastructure followed. Swap desks went where the dollars were, and stayed.

### The venues and infrastructure

- **ICE Futures Europe** lists Brent crude, the world's main oil benchmark, alongside UK natural gas, SONIA interest rate futures, gilt futures, FTSE 100 futures and options, and the London cocoa, coffee and sugar contracts.
- **The London Metal Exchange** sets global prices for copper, aluminium and nickel. Owned by Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing since 2012, it still trades in its famous open-outcry ring.
- **LCH**, owned by the London Stock Exchange Group, is the giant of swap clearing. In 2025 it cleared **97.4%** of core US dollar interest rate swaps, **95.2%** of euro swaps and effectively all sterling swaps.
- **ICE Clear Europe** and **LME Clear** clear the exchange-traded contracts.

### Who regulates what

The **Financial Conduct Authority** regulates conduct, trading venues and retail products, including the CFD restrictions of Lesson 10, and polices market abuse. The **Bank of England** supervises clearing houses and watches financial stability, while its **Prudential Regulation Authority** supervises banks and insurers. Since Brexit, the EU's derivatives rules live on as **UK EMIR**, covering clearing, reporting and margin.

### The clearing tug of war after Brexit

The EU dislikes so much euro clearing happening outside its borders, but it cannot yet do without London. The European Commission has extended its recognition of UK clearing houses until **30 June 2028** to buy time. Meanwhile, an EU rule live since June 2025 requires EU firms to hold active clearing accounts inside the EU. It nudged Eurex's share of euro swap clearing up from 2.6% to **4.8%** in 2025, while LCH kept the rest.

### The pension connection: liability-driven investment

UK defined benefit pension funds hold huge interest rate and inflation swaps, plus gilts financed with repo, to match their long-dated promises to pensioners: the strategy called **liability-driven investment (LDI)**. In September 2022 a sudden surge in gilt yields triggered collateral calls they could not meet fast enough, and forced gilt selling fed the spiral until the Bank of England intervened (Lessons 7 and 13). Funds now hold far bigger buffers: regulators expect LDI funds to withstand a rise in yields of at least 2.5 percentage points.

### Retail: the home of spread betting

Spread betting was born in London in 1974, when IG Index began offering bets on the price of gold to British investors who could not easily own it. London-listed IG Group, CMC Markets and Plus500 are now among the world's largest retail CFD providers. Listed options, by contrast, remain a niche product for UK individuals.

### From LIBOR to SONIA

LIBOR was set by banks reporting the rates they claimed they could borrow at, and the 2012 rigging scandal showed how easily that could be gamed (Lesson 13). Sterling markets moved to **SONIA**, an overnight rate built from real transactions and administered by the Bank of England. ISDA's fallback protocol moved trillions of dollars of existing contracts across, and the last synthetic LIBOR settings, for the dollar, ended in September 2024.

### What to watch

- Whether more euro clearing moves to the EU before the June 2028 deadline.
- Pension fund resilience as gilt markets stay volatile.
- How the FCA balances retail protection with the competitiveness of London-listed trading firms.

## India: the retail options giant, deliberately cooled

India built the world's most intensely traded retail options market and has spent the last two years deliberately cooling it. In the first quarter of 2024 alone, Indian exchanges traded more than **34.8 billion** equity index options, more than 73% of all the futures and options traded on every exchange in the world.

### Two regulators, plus the tax authority

- **SEBI** regulates exchange-traded equity derivatives on NSE and BSE, and commodity derivatives on MCX and NCDEX since the commodities regulator merged into it in 2015.
- **The Reserve Bank of India** governs interest rate and currency derivatives, most of which trade over the counter with banks and are cleared by CCIL. In 2024 it insisted that users of exchange-traded currency derivatives must have a genuine underlying exposure, which shrank that market sharply.
- **The government** shapes trading through the **securities transaction tax (STT)**, charged on every derivatives trade regardless of profit.

### The venues

- **NSE** dominates index options on the Nifty 50 and Bank Nifty. Its weekly contracts expire on Tuesdays, and BSE's on Thursdays, after SEBI settled a turf war over expiry days from 1 September 2025.
- **BSE** revived its Sensex options from 2023 and became NSE's main rival.
- **MCX** trades crude oil, natural gas, gold and silver, and launched electricity futures in July 2025.
- **NSE International Exchange** in GIFT City lists GIFT Nifty, a dollar-denominated Nifty future for offshore investors that moved there from Singapore in 2023.

### How the boom happened, and the crackdown

Tiny contract sizes, an index expiry on almost every weekday, discount brokers with slick apps, and a wave of new investors after the pandemic turned options into a national pastime. Most of the activity was retail money buying cheap, short-dated options, often on expiry day itself. NSE topped the world's derivatives exchanges by number of contracts every year from 2019 to 2024, and the crackdown was large enough to dent global statistics: in the first seven months of 2025 Brazil's B3, whose contracts are even smaller, overtook it.

The steps were deliberate and phased. From November 2024, larger minimum contract sizes and one weekly expiry per exchange; from early 2025, upfront premiums and intraday position checks; through 2025, delta-based limits and a pre-open auction; and from April 2026, a higher transaction tax, raised from 0.02% to **0.05%** on futures and from 0.10% to **0.15%** on options premiums. The results are in Lesson 10: fewer traders and smaller total losses, but still close to nine in ten individuals losing money.

### The Jane Street case

In July 2025 SEBI accused the US trading firm Jane Street of manipulating the Bank Nifty index between January 2023 and March 2025. It alleged that the firm bought bank shares and futures in the morning to push the index up while building large positions in index options that would profit as the index fell back, then sold the shares later in the day. It ordered the firm to deposit **₹4,843.57 crore** and briefly barred it from trading. Jane Street denied wrongdoing, deposited the money and resumed trading, and appealed to the Securities Appellate Tribunal in September 2025, arguing that it had been denied the documents needed for its defence. In October 2026 the tribunal finished hearing arguments over its demand for SEBI's order logs and was due to return to the matter on 21 October, so check for later rulings.

The case matters well beyond one firm, because it asks where aggressive but legal hedging ends and manipulation begins: a question at the heart of expiry-day markets everywhere, and of Lesson 9's dealer hedging flows.

### What makes India different

- **Retail money dominates index options**, the reverse of the institution-led US futures markets.
- **Policy works through product design and taxes**, not just disclosure: SEBI changes contract sizes, expiry days and margins directly.
- **Interest rate and currency hedging remain largely in banks' hands** under the RBI, so India's corporate hedging market is smaller than its trading volumes suggest. The overnight MIBOR rate that floats India's swaps is itself being replaced: since July 2025 a new secured benchmark, **SORR**, built from repo transactions, has been published alongside it.

### What to watch

- The Securities Appellate Tribunal's handling of the Jane Street appeal.
- Whether SEBI pushes trading towards longer-dated options and away from expiry-day speculation.
- The growth of electricity futures, interest rate derivatives and GIFT City as genuine hedging markets.

## Check yourself

**1. A US company wants to hedge (a) an interest rate swap, (b) a credit default swap on a single company, and (c) S&P 500 index options. Which regulator oversees each?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

(a) **CFTC**: interest rate swaps are swaps. (b) **SEC**: a single-name CDS is a security-based swap. (c) **SEC**: options on stock indices are securities options. Options on S&P 500 *futures*, by contrast, would be the CFTC's.

</details>

**2. Why does it matter to the EU that LCH clears 95.2% of euro swaps?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because a clearing house holds the margin and absorbs the defaults for the trades it clears, a crisis in euro swaps would be managed by a London institution, supervised by the Bank of England, outside the EU's direct control. That is why the EU only grants UK clearing houses time-limited recognition (now to June 2028) and requires EU firms to hold active accounts inside the EU.

</details>

**3. A Nifty 50 future covers 65 units of the index. With the Nifty at 22,500, what is one contract worth? Why would SEBI care about that number?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

65 × 22,500 = **₹14.6 lakh**. SEBI sets lot sizes so that a contract is worth at least ₹15 lakh when they are reviewed, deliberately pricing out the smallest traders; as the index moves, the value drifts until the next review.

</details>

**4. What does the securities transaction tax do to a trader who buys and sells options ten times a day?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

STT is charged on every trade, profit or loss, so it scales with **activity**, not with success. At 0.15% of the premium on each sale, a trader who churns the same capital many times a day pays the tax many times over, which is precisely the behaviour the 2026 increase was designed to discourage.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 12

- The **US** leads in exchange-traded futures and options, under **two regulators** split by history; Treasury clearing (December 2026) and **prediction markets** are the live issues.
- The **UK** books **half** of global OTC interest rate trading and clears almost all swaps through **LCH**; EU recognition runs to **June 2028**; LDI funds now hold far bigger buffers.
- **India** built the largest retail options market and cooled it through **product design and taxes**; the **Jane Street** appeal tests where hedging ends and manipulation begins.
- **SOFR, SONIA and SORR**: all three markets have moved, or are moving, to benchmarks built from real transactions.

Lessons 12 and 13 put all of this in motion: twenty-four episodes, from 1973 to 2026, each teaching one of the ideas you have now learned.

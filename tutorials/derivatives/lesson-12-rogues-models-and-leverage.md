# Lesson 12: Rogues, Models and Leverage, 1973 to 2007

*Nine episodes, each a case study in an idea from Lessons 2 to 9: from the birth of the modern options market to the first tremors of the 2008 crisis. Together they explain why today's market has the rules, the plumbing and the instincts it has.*

---

## Why stories, not just rules

Every rule in the derivatives market was written in response to something. Clearing houses for swaps exist because of 2008; negative balance protection exists because of a January morning in 2015; banks separate their trading desks from the people who check them because of one man in Singapore. Learn the stories and the rules stop looking arbitrary. They look like scar tissue.

Lessons 12 and 13 tell twenty-four such stories. Each one isolates a concept you have already met, and each ends with what the market learned.

## 1. 1973: Options get a price tag

**Concepts:** listed options, the pricing formula, volatility

On 26 April 1973, the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened for business, trading call options on just **16** shares in a converted smoking lounge of the Chicago Board of Trade. Until then, options had been traded informally between dealers, with no standard terms and no clearing house. The same spring, Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published the formula Lesson 3 described, and Robert Merton published his own derivation. Within a few years traders on the floor were carrying calculators programmed with the formula.

The timing was not an accident. A standardised, cleared market made prices visible; a formula made them consistent. Scholes and Merton won the Nobel prize in economics in 1997; Black, who had died in 1995, could not share it.

> **The lesson:** Once there was a formula, everything in it could be looked up except one thing. Volatility became the quantity that option traders actually trade (Lesson 4), and it has been ever since.

## 2. 1987: Black Monday and portfolio insurance

**Concepts:** dynamic hedging, feedback loops, the skew

In the mid-1980s, many large funds bought **portfolio insurance**: not an insurance policy, but a computer strategy that mimicked a put option by selling stock index futures as prices fell and buying them back as prices rose. It was delta hedging, from Lesson 5, run in reverse by thousands of investors at once.

On **Monday 19 October 1987**, after a week of falling prices, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell **22.6%** in a single day, still its worst day ever in percentage terms. As prices dropped, portfolio insurance programmes sold billions of dollars of index futures, which dragged the futures below the shares, which prompted arbitrageurs to sell shares, which pushed prices down further and triggered more selling by the insurance programmes. Nobody intended to crash the market. The hedging rules, followed mechanically by enough people, did it for them.

> **The lesson:** Dynamic hedging creates feedback loops (Lesson 9). Ever since 1987, stock index options have charged extra for crash protection: the skew of Lesson 4 is, in effect, the market's memory of that Monday. Exchanges also introduced circuit breakers that pause trading after very large falls.

## 3. 1993: Metallgesellschaft's hedge that bled cash

**Concepts:** funding liquidity, stack-and-roll hedging, the margin spiral

The US arm of the German industrial group Metallgesellschaft sold customers heating oil and petrol at fixed prices for up to **ten years** ahead, about 160 million barrels in all. To hedge, it bought short-dated oil futures and rolled them every month, a "stack and roll". As long as oil prices held up, everything balanced.

In late 1993 oil prices fell sharply. The futures lost money immediately, and the losses were collected in cash every evening as variation margin. The matching gains, on fixed-price contracts with customers, were just as real but would arrive only as oil was delivered over the following decade. To make matters worse, the oil market had moved into contango, so every monthly roll cost money too (the [commodity guide](?slug=commodity-markets&lesson=lesson-06-the-forward-curve) explains roll yield). The parent company's new management closed the positions with losses of about **$1.3 billion**, and needed a rescue from its banks. Economists still argue about whether the hedge was flawed or whether closing it was the real mistake.

> **The lesson:** A hedge can be sound on paper and still kill you through cash flow: the second big idea of this guide, in its purest form. Hedgers now plan for **funding liquidity** as well as price risk.

Leveraged bets on rates staying low were savaged. **Orange County**, California, had borrowed heavily to buy notes whose value fell sharply as rates rose; its fund lost about **$1.6 billion** and the county filed for bankruptcy in December 1994, then the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history. **Procter & Gamble** lost **$157 million** on two complex interest rate swaps sold by Bankers Trust, whose payments were geared to rise dramatically if rates moved, and sued, arguing that it had been misled about the risks. Taped conversations of Bankers Trust salespeople, released during the litigation, did lasting damage to the industry's reputation.

> **The lesson:** Leverage on interest rates multiplies the sensitivity of Lesson 7, and complex products can hide how much. Ever since, regulators have asked not just whether a derivative was priced fairly, but whether it was **suitable** for the buyer.

On 17 January 1995 an earthquake struck Kobe, and the Nikkei tumbled. Leeson doubled down, buying more futures in an attempt to prop up the market and recover his losses. By late February they had reached **£827 million**, more than the bank's entire capital. **Barings**, founded in **1762** and banker to governments for two centuries, collapsed on 26 February 1995 and was sold to the Dutch group ING for **£1**. Leeson had been running both the trading desk and the back office that recorded and settled its trades, so nobody independent was checking his numbers.

> **The lesson:** Operational risk can be as deadly as market risk. **Never let one person both trade and keep the books** (Lesson 9's hidden concentration). The separation of front office and back office is now a basic rule of every trading business.

## 6. 1998: LTCM, when genius met leverage

**Concepts:** leverage, correlations going to one, illiquidity, "too interconnected to fail"

Long-Term Capital Management was the most admired hedge fund in the world. Its partners included Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, who had won the Nobel prize the year before, and its strategy was **convergence trading**: buying securities that looked cheap and selling similar ones that looked dear, and waiting for the gaps to close. The gaps were tiny, so the fund used enormous leverage, with more than **$1 trillion** of derivatives notional on a few billion dollars of capital.

In August 1998 Russia defaulted on its debt, and investors everywhere fled to the safest, most liquid assets. Almost every one of LTCM's trades was a bet that such panics would fade, so every one moved against the fund at once: the "diversified" portfolio turned out to be one big bet on calm. It lost **44%** in August alone, and by 22 September its capital had fallen from **$4.7 billion** at the start of the year to about **$0.6 billion**. Its positions were so large, and so entangled with the world's banks, that a fire sale threatened them all. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York brought **14** banks and brokers together, and they injected **$3.6 billion** to take control and wind the fund down slowly.

> **The lesson:** Leverage, correlation and illiquidity are each manageable alone and lethal together (Lesson 9's model risk). LTCM also introduced the idea that a firm could be **too interconnected to fail**, which would return, at far larger scale, in 2008.

## 7. 2001: Enron, the trader that marked its own homework

**Concepts:** mark-to-model, opacity, unregulated markets

Enron began as a gas pipeline company and reinvented itself as an energy trading house, running its own online trading platform, EnronOnline, on which it was the counterparty to every trade. A law passed in 2000, the Commodity Futures Modernization Act, had exempted much electronic energy trading from oversight. Enron valued its long-term energy contracts with its own models, booking decades of expected profits on the day a deal was signed, and hid debt in off-balance-sheet vehicles.

When questions about its accounts mounted in late 2001, counterparties stopped trading with it, its credit rating was cut, and the business evaporated within weeks. Enron filed for bankruptcy on **2 December 2001**, then the largest in US history, and took its auditor, Arthur Andersen, down with it.

> **The lesson:** Marking illiquid contracts to a model rather than a market lets a firm show profits that may never exist. For a trader, **trust is the real capital**: once counterparties doubt you, you are finished. Independent price verification of hard-to-value contracts is now a basic control (Lesson 14).

## 8. 2006: Amaranth's bet on natural gas

**Concepts:** concentration, liquidity, position limits

Amaranth Advisors was a multi-strategy hedge fund, but by 2006 most of its risk sat with one young energy trader, Brian Hunter, who had built enormous positions in natural gas futures spreads, betting that winter gas would become much dearer relative to spring gas. The positions were so large that Amaranth was, in some contracts, most of the market.

In September 2006 the spreads moved against it. Because it *was* the market, there was nobody to sell to without moving prices further against itself. The fund lost about **$6 billion** in a few weeks, roughly two-thirds of its assets, and its positions were sold off to JPMorgan and Citadel at a deep discount. A US Senate investigation later found that Amaranth had moved much of its trading to an electronic exchange outside the reach of position limits.

> **The lesson:** When you are the market, you cannot get out at the market price. Concentration turns liquidity from an assumption into a fantasy, which is why regulators impose **position limits** and why electronic energy markets were brought under fuller oversight.

That announcement hit the plumbing first. Banks, unsure which of them held the toxic securities, stopped lending to each other, and the gap between the rate at which banks lent to each other and the rate on government bills jumped overnight. Within weeks Northern Rock, in Britain, suffered the first run on a UK bank in more than 140 years. The gauge in the chart would not settle until the crisis of 2008 had run its course.

> **The lesson:** Derivatives prices often signal trouble before anything else does, because they are where people go to hedge. And an asset that only a model can price is worth what someone will pay for it on the worst day, which may be nothing.

## Check yourself

**1. A portfolio insurance programme mimics a put option on a £1 billion portfolio. The market falls 5%, and the programme's rules say its hedge ratio should rise from 0.3 to 0.5. What does it do, and what does it do to the market?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Its hedge must grow from 0.3 × £1 billion = £300 million to 0.5 × £950 million = £475 million, so it sells about **£175 million** more of index futures, into a falling market. If many funds follow the same rule, their selling pushes prices down further, which raises their hedge ratios again: the feedback loop of 1987.

</details>

**2. Metallgesellschaft hedged 160 million barrels with short-dated futures. If oil falls $5 a barrel, how much variation margin must it pay, and when do the offsetting gains arrive?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

160 million × $5 = **$800 million**, in cash, as the price falls. The matching gains on its fixed-price contracts arrive only as the oil is delivered over the next ten years. Economically hedged; in cash, deeply exposed.

</details>

**3. LTCM's capital fell from $4.7 billion to $0.6 billion. If its derivatives notional was about $1 trillion, roughly how many times its capital was that at the start of 1998, and in late September?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

$1,000bn ÷ $4.7bn ≈ **210 times** at the start of the year; $1,000bn ÷ $0.6bn ≈ **1,700 times** by late September. Notional overstates the risk (Lesson 1), but the trend is the point: as losses shrank the capital, the same positions became ever more leveraged, which is why the fund could not survive waiting for its trades to converge.

</details>

**4. Which single control would have stopped Barings from being destroyed by one trader?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**Segregation of duties**: someone independent of the trader recording, reconciling and settling his trades. Leeson ran both the front office and the back office in Singapore, so the secret account went unnoticed until the losses exceeded the bank's capital.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 13

- **1973**: a cleared options market plus a pricing formula turned volatility into the thing that is traded.
- **1987**: portfolio insurance showed how **dynamic hedging creates feedback loops**, and left the market with a permanent **skew**.
- **1993**: Metallgesellschaft showed that a hedge can be **right on paper and fatal in cash**.
- **1994**: leverage on interest rates, and the question of **suitability**.
- **1995**: Barings: **never let one person trade and keep the books**.
- **1998**: LTCM: **leverage, correlation and illiquidity together**, and the first firm "too interconnected to fail".
- **2001 and 2006**: Enron's **mark-to-model** profits and Amaranth's **concentration**.
- **2007**: derivatives and funding markets **signalled the crisis first**.

Lesson 13 picks up the story in 2008, when the lessons shifted from rogue traders towards plumbing.

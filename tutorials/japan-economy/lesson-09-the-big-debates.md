# Lesson 9: The Big Debates

*Five questions that economists, investors and politicians are arguing about right now. None has a settled answer, which is exactly why they are worth understanding.*

---

## Debate 1: Is deflation really dead?

**The optimists** point to three straight years of 5% wage settlements, inflation at or above 2% for most of the period since 2022, firms that now routinely raise prices, and record inflation expectations in the BOJ's surveys of companies. Japan's price system, frozen for a generation, has started to move.

**The sceptics** note that core inflation has been below 2% in every month of 2026, that much of the earlier inflation came from a weak yen and expensive energy rather than domestic demand, and that household spending is barely growing. Strip out the imported part, they say, and the home-grown inflation that would sustain itself is thinner than the headline suggests.

**The honest answer** is that the deflationary *mindset* has broken, but the new equilibrium is not yet proven. The test is whether wages and service prices keep rising once the energy shock and the weak yen fade, without the government holding prices down with subsidies. Watch the core-core rate and services inflation, not the headline.

The arithmetic that kept Japan safe still holds. Nominal GDP has grown by roughly 4% to 5% a year since fiscal 2023, while the average rate paid on the government's existing debt is still only about **1%**, because so much of it was issued near zero and its average maturity is over nine years. That gap is why the debt ratio has been falling.

This chart is the key to the debate. The average rate on all outstanding bonds (red) moves slowly, because only the bonds that mature each year are refinanced at today's yields. For thirty years market yields (green) sat *below* that average, so every refinancing pulled it down, from over 7% in the 1980s to 0.76% in fiscal 2022. Now the relationship has flipped. Every bond that matures is refinanced at 2% to 4% instead of near zero, so interest costs will climb for years *even if yields stop rising*. The average had already risen to 0.98% by March 2026.

The fiscal 2026 budget sets aside **¥13.1 trillion** for interest, and total debt service, including repayments, takes a quarter of central government spending. The budget assumed a long-term rate of 3% when it was drawn up; for fiscal 2027 the Ministry of Finance is reported to be considering 3.8%.

The BOJ faces its own squeeze. It now pays interest on the hundreds of trillions of yen that banks hold in their accounts with it, while much of its bond portfolio, bought at the bottom, still yields less than 1%. The profits it normally hands to the Treasury will shrink and may turn into losses.

### The nightmare scenario

The nightmare is a loss of confidence. If investors began demanding much higher yields to hold JGBs, the BOJ would face an impossible choice: buy bonds to cap yields, creating money that would weaken the yen and fuel inflation, or let yields rise and risk a fiscal and banking shock. Economists call this **fiscal dominance**: the point at which a central bank's decisions are dictated by the government's debts.

Japan is not there, and its defences remain formidable. The debt is held at home, the country is one of the world's largest net creditors, tax revenue is at a record, and the IMF projects the debt ratio to keep falling. But the January 2026 wobble over the food tax and the September breach of 3% on 10-year yields show that markets are now scrutinising fiscal policy in a way they did not for 25 years.

Japan still runs a large current-account surplus, but look at what it is made of. Until 2010 the surplus came mostly from selling goods (green). Now it comes almost entirely from **primary income** (purple): dividends, interest and profits on Japan's huge stock of overseas investments. That matters for the yen because much of that income is **reinvested abroad** rather than converted into yen. A trade surplus forces exporters to sell dollars for yen; an investment-income surplus does not.

Three more outflows add to the pressure.

- **Energy.** Japan imports almost all its energy, so every oil shock means more yen sold.
- **The digital deficit.** Japan pays far more to mostly American firms for cloud computing, software, online advertising and content than it earns from such services.
- **Households going global.** Since the expanded NISA scheme launched in 2024, households have been pouring savings into global equity funds, which means selling yen.

Put all of this together and the yen's **real effective exchange rate**, which adjusts for inflation across all trading partners, has fallen to levels last seen in the mid-1960s. Optimists argue that a currency this undervalued must eventually recover, especially if the BOJ keeps raising rates and the Fed eventually cuts again. Pessimists reply that the structural outflows will persist and that a government focused on spending will keep the pressure on. The joint US-Japanese intervention in July shows that both Tokyo and Washington regard a yen beyond 160 as dangerous, which in practice puts a soft ceiling on how weak it can get, at least for now.

## Debate 4: Are households winning or losing?

Nominal wages are rising at their fastest pace in a generation, and real wages turned positive in 2026 after years of decline. But part of that improvement came from energy subsidies holding prices down, and consumption, which makes up more than half of GDP, has been flat.

Inflation also redistributes wealth.

Japanese households keep about **45%** of their financial assets, roughly ¥1,100 trillion, in cash and bank deposits. Each year of 2% to 3% inflation quietly erodes the real value of those savings. The chart also shows the shift under way: shares and investment funds have risen from about 14% of household assets in 2013 to about 27%, helped by rising markets and the NISA scheme.

Pensions are the other squeeze. Under a formula introduced in 2004 to keep the system solvent, pensions are deliberately raised by *less* than inflation. Older voters, who are numerous and vote reliably, feel the pinch most, which helps explain the political appetite for tax cuts and subsidies.

## Debate 5: Can a shrinking country keep growing?

Japan's population is falling by several hundred thousand people a year, and its working-age population is shrinking faster still. That caps potential growth at around 0.5%. But it has a silver lining: **labour shortages are the main reason wages are finally rising**, and they push firms to invest in automation, software and AI. Business investment plans have been strong, and the state is spending heavily to rebuild a domestic semiconductor industry.

Here lies a revealing contrast. Japanese firms such as **Tokyo Electron** and **Advantest** sell essential equipment for making and testing the chips behind the AI boom, yet Japan rents most of its cloud and AI services from American providers. One estimate suggests that if 30 million Japanese subscribed to foreign AI services at ¥3,000 a month, outflows would rise by more than ¥1 trillion a year. Japan sells shovels in the AI gold rush, but pays rent to those who own the mines.

Whether Japan can capture more of that value, while easing labour shortages through automation and, more controversially, immigration, will shape its growth for decades.

204 × (0.01 − 0.04) ÷ 1.04 ≈ −5.9 points, plus the 2-point deficit, gives about **−3.9 points**. The ratio falls, to about 200%. The danger is gradual: as the average rate creeps towards 2% or 3% and inflation fades, the first term shrinks towards zero.

**3. Households hold about ¥1,130 trillion in cash and deposits earning close to nothing. If inflation is 2.5% a year, roughly how much purchasing power do those savings lose in a year?**

1,130 × 0.025 ≈ **¥28 trillion a year**, about 4% of GDP. Inflation acts like a silent tax on savers, which is part of why the government is keen for households to invest through NISA, and part of why older savers feel poorer even as wages rise.

## What to carry into Lesson 10

- **Deflation:** the mindset has broken; the new equilibrium is unproven until home-grown inflation survives without the weak yen and subsidies.
- **Debt:** falling as a share of GDP because **growth beats interest**, but the average rate on the debt will **ramp up for years** as bonds are refinanced.
- **Yen:** weak because of the **rate gap** plus **structural outflows**: reinvested investment income, energy, the digital deficit and NISA.
- **Households:** higher wages, but **45% of savings in cash** and pensions that lag inflation.
- **Growth:** a shrinking workforce caps growth but forces investment; Japan **sells the shovels** of the AI boom and **rents the mines**.

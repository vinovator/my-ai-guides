# Lesson 3: 2006 to 2012, False Dawn, Financial Crisis and the Super-Strong Yen

*A recovery nobody felt, a carry trade run from living rooms, a global crash that hit Japan harder than the countries that caused it, and a yen so strong it threatened to hollow out Japanese industry.*

---

## What was pressing

In 2006, whether deflation was finally beaten and policy could return to normal. From 2008, how to survive a global collapse in demand and a soaring yen. And in 2011, how to recover from the worst natural disaster in Japan's modern history.

---

## A recovery nobody felt

By the mid-2000s Japan was enjoying its longest post-war expansion: **73 months**, from February 2002 to February 2008. It was powered by exports to a booming China and a debt-fuelled America, helped along by a cheap yen. Profits recovered and the banks were finally clean.

But wages barely rose, and the Japanese press coined a phrase for it: *jikkan naki keiki kaifuku*, a recovery without the feeling of recovery. Companies scarred by the 1990s kept the gains as cash rather than paying them out.

Judging the economy strong enough, the BOJ under **Toshihiko Fukui** ended quantitative easing in March 2006, raised its rate to 0.25% in July 2006 and to 0.5% in February 2007. Core inflation was hovering around zero. Critics argued that the BOJ was once again leaving the party too early.

---

## The carry trade and "Mrs Watanabe"

With Japanese rates near zero and American rates at 5.25%, borrowing in yen to buy higher-yielding assets abroad became one of the world's most popular trades. Hedge funds did it on a vast scale. So did Japanese households trading currencies from home, buying Australian and New Zealand dollar deposits and leveraged foreign-exchange accounts. The foreign press nicknamed them **"Mrs Watanabe"**, after a common Japanese surname.

Their collective selling of yen helped push it to about 124 per dollar in June 2007, and it was weaker still against the euro. Then the gap that had made it all work disappeared.

Read the two panels together. While the gap between American and Japanese rates was around 5 percentage points, borrowing yen was nearly free money and the yen drifted weaker. When the US Federal Reserve cut its rate to near zero in December 2008, the gap collapsed, the reason to borrow yen vanished, and positions were unwound in a rush. Unwinding means buying back yen to repay the loans, so the yen soared.

## The crisis arrives from abroad

Japan's banks had little exposure to American subprime mortgages, so many expected Japan to escape the 2008 crisis lightly. Instead it suffered one of the deepest recessions in the rich world.

The reason was the shape of the economy. Japan had grown in the 2000s by exporting cars, machinery and electronics to exactly the customers who stopped buying. In early 2009 its exports were running at roughly half their level of a year earlier. Real GDP fell by more than 5% in 2009, and the drop from the start of 2008 to the trough was about 8%, against about 3.5% in America and about 6% in the euro area. Deflation returned too, with core consumer prices falling by about 1% in both 2009 and 2010.

### The yen goes through the roof

Three forces sent the yen soaring at the worst possible moment.

1. **Panic** triggered the safe-haven reflex: Japanese investors brought money home.
2. **Carry trades** were unwound in a rush, which meant buying yen.
3. **The rate gap** that had held the yen down simply vanished once the Fed cut to near zero.

The yen strengthened from about 124 per dollar in 2007 to a record of about **75** in October 2011. For exporters this was brutal: $10,000 of overseas sales that was worth ¥1.24 million in mid-2007 was worth only about ¥757,000 four years later. Some firms responded the only way they could, by moving factories abroad.

## 3/11

On **11 March 2011** a magnitude 9.0 earthquake and tsunami struck north-eastern Japan, leaving about 18,000 people dead or missing and causing meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant.

Perversely, the yen hit a record high within days, as markets bet that Japanese insurers and investors would sell foreign assets and bring money home to pay for reconstruction. On 18 March the G7 intervened jointly to weaken it, the first coordinated intervention in more than a decade.

The longer-lasting damage came through energy. As the country's nuclear reactors were shut down for safety reviews, Japan had to import vast quantities of liquefied natural gas and oil to keep the lights on.

In 2011 Japan recorded its first annual trade deficit since 1980 on the customs measure, and the balance of goods trade stayed weak or negative for years. Quietly, this ended Japan's era of dependable trade surpluses. A decade later it would matter greatly for the yen: a country that no longer earns a surplus from selling goods generates less natural demand for its own currency.

---

## Policy under pressure

**Masaaki Shirakawa**, BOJ governor from 2008, was a respected scholar of central banking who doubted that money creation alone could cure deflation rooted in an ageing population and weak productivity. Under him the BOJ:

- cut its rate to 0.3% and then 0.1% in late 2008;
- launched **"comprehensive monetary easing"** in October 2010, which included buying stock-market funds (ETFs) and property funds (J-REITs) for the first time; and
- adopted a 1% inflation **"goal"** in February 2012.

To critics, each step looked too small and too late. The Ministry of Finance meanwhile spent heavily trying to hold back the currency, including a record single-day intervention of about **¥8.1 trillion** on 31 October 2011, with only fleeting effect.

### Politics

Japan churned through six prime ministers in six years after 2006. In 2009 the Democratic Party of Japan (DPJ) took power, ending more than half a century of almost unbroken rule by the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP). In 2012 the DPJ prime minister **Yoshihiko Noda** struck a deal with the opposition to double the consumption tax in two steps, to 8% and then 10%, to pay for the rising costs of an ageing society. He then called an election and lost it in a landslide to the LDP under a returning **Shinzo Abe**.

---

## What it left behind

By late 2012 there was a broad sense that some of Japan's problems were self-inflicted: a central bank that seemed too cautious, a yen that looked absurdly strong, and an industrial base drifting abroad. The energy shock had also ended the era of reliable trade surpluses. The public, business leaders and many economists were ready for a radical experiment.

## Check yourself

**1. In June 2007 the yen was about 124 per dollar; in October 2011 about 75.7. A Japanese exporter's US sales stayed at $10,000. By what percentage did their value in yen fall?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

At 124: ¥1,240,000. At 75.7: ¥757,000. The fall is 1 − 75.7 ÷ 124 ≈ **39%**. Same sale, same customer, nearly two fifths less revenue at home.

</details>

**2. In 2007 a hedge fund borrows yen at 0.5% and invests in dollars earning 5.25%. Roughly how much does it earn a year if the exchange rate does not move? And what happens if the yen strengthens 10% over the year?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

The carry is 5.25% − 0.5% = **4.75%** a year. But if the yen strengthens by 10%, the dollars buy about 10% fewer yen when the loan is repaid, which wipes out the carry and leaves a loss of roughly **5%** of the position. With leverage of five times, that is a quarter of the fund's capital. Small, steady gains and occasional large, sudden losses are the signature of the carry trade.

</details>

**3. Why did the yen hit a record high days after the 2011 earthquake, when Japan had just suffered a disaster?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Markets expected Japanese insurers, companies and investors to sell foreign assets and repatriate the money to pay claims and rebuild. That means selling dollars and buying yen. The safe-haven reflex works through Japanese money coming home, whatever the cause of the alarm, even when the alarm is in Japan itself.

</details>

**4. Japan's banks barely touched subprime mortgages. Why did its economy shrink more than America's in 2008 and 2009?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because its growth had come from exporting manufactured goods to America, Europe and China, and global demand for cars, machinery and electronics collapsed. Exports roughly halved. At the same time the yen soared as carry trades unwound and the rate gap closed, which squeezed exporters further. The shock came through trade and the currency, not through the banks.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 4

- The 2002 to 2008 recovery was long but **wageless**, and the BOJ again raised rates with inflation near zero.
- The **carry trade** pushed the yen weak while the rate gap was wide; when the Fed cut to zero, it **unwound** and the yen soared to about **75**.
- Japan's export-heavy economy made it one of the **hardest-hit** by 2008 and 2009.
- **Fukushima** turned Japan into a large energy importer and ended its era of dependable **trade surpluses**.
- By 2012, voters and markets were ready for something radical.

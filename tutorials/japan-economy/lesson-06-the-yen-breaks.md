# Lesson 6: 2022 to 2024, the World Inflates and the Yen Breaks

*A global inflation shock that Japan had not caused, a currency in free fall, a bond-market siege, the wage breakthrough the BOJ had waited a generation for, and a single August day that showed how much of the world runs on cheap yen.*

---

## What was pressing

A global inflation shock that Japan had not caused, a yen in free fall as other central banks raced to raise rates, and an intense debate over whether Japan's new inflation was the "bad" kind that would fade or the "good" kind it had sought for 25 years.

---

## The rate gap explodes

In 2022 Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent energy and food prices soaring, and the US Federal Reserve raised its rate from near zero to over 4% within a year, on the way to 5.5% in 2023. The BOJ held firm at −0.1% and kept its yield cap.

Governor Kuroda's reasoning was that Japan's inflation was imported and temporary, and that tightening into a cost shock would crush a fragile recovery before wages had a chance to respond. The logic was defensible. But markets did the arithmetic: the gap between American and Japanese rates was the widest in decades, and money poured out of yen.

### The yen breaks

The yen slid from about 115 per dollar at the start of 2022 to almost 152 in October, its weakest since 1990. In September and October the Ministry of Finance intervened to *buy* yen for the first time since 1998, spending about **¥9.2 trillion**. It bought time, not a change of direction, because it did nothing to close the rate gap that was driving the selling.

The weak yen amplified the energy shock. Japan ran a record trade deficit of nearly ¥20 trillion in 2022 on the customs measure, and core inflation reached **4.2% in January 2023**, the highest since 1981.

In mid-2022 Kuroda remarked that households were becoming more accepting of price rises, provoked a public backlash and apologised. The episode captured the tension perfectly: the inflation the BOJ had wanted for decades had arrived, but in a form that was making people poorer. Notice too how the purple line, core-core inflation, rose later and stayed up longer than the orange one. Once energy prices stopped rising, price increases were spreading to services and processed goods. That was the first hint that something more lasting was under way.

---

## Yield curve control under siege

As global yields rose, holding Japan's 10-year yield at 0.25% required the BOJ to buy ever larger quantities of bonds. Hedge funds bet that it would have to give up, hoping at last to win the widowmaker trade. The BOJ came to own so much of some bond issues that they barely traded, and the market stopped working properly: the 10-year bond yielded less than bonds a year shorter and a year longer, a kink created entirely by the central bank.

In December 2022 the BOJ unexpectedly widened the band to 0.5% either side of zero, insisting that this was not a rate rise. The following month its bond purchases hit a monthly record of more than ¥20 trillion as it defended the new line. (The ceiling's progress is in Lesson 5's YCC chart.)

---

## Ueda and the slow unwind

In April 2023 **Kazuo Ueda**, an economist and the first post-war governor drawn from academia, took over. He moved methodically, loosening YCC in July and October 2023 until the 1% ceiling became a soft "reference" rather than a hard cap. His overriding concern was not to repeat the premature exits of 2000 and 2006, so he waited for proof that **wages**, not just import prices, were driving inflation.

---

## The wage breakthrough

The proof came from the spring wage negotiations, the **shunto**, in which large companies and unions agree annual pay rises that set the tone for the whole economy.

For two decades the shunto had delivered roughly 2% a year, and most of that was automatic seniority pay that rises with age rather than a genuine increase in the wage scale. In 2023 the headline settlement was about **3.6%**, the highest in three decades. In 2024 it was **5.1%**, the highest since 1991. Labour shortages had finally given workers bargaining power, and companies facing rising costs were, for the first time in a generation, willing to pass them on.

## The end of the experiment

On **19 March 2024** the BOJ raised its policy rate for the first time in 17 years, ending eight years of negative rates. In the same decision it abandoned yield curve control and stopped buying stock and property funds, closing the most aggressive monetary experiment ever run by a major central bank.

Markets took it calmly, and then the yen *weakened* further. The move was tiny next to the gap with American rates, and the BOJ signalled that policy would stay accommodative. A rise from −0.1% to about 0.1% does nothing to the attraction of a 5.5% dollar rate.

### The yen at 160

By late April 2024 the yen had passed 160 per dollar, and in early July it touched about 162, its weakest since December 1986. The Ministry of Finance intervened in late April and early May and again in July, spending about **¥15.3 trillion** over the year, then a record for buying yen. Tourists flocked to Japan for bargains and exporters' profits soared, but households faced another squeeze on real incomes.

On **22 February 2024** the Nikkei finally closed above its December 1989 peak. This was not a new bubble.

- **Profits had risen strongly**, and the weak yen inflated overseas earnings.
- **A quiet governance revolution was under way.** In 2023 the Tokyo Stock Exchange asked listed companies to show they were earning more than their cost of capital, especially the many trading below the book value of their assets. Companies responded with record share buybacks and dividends. The reforms begun in 2014 and 2015 (Lesson 4) were bearing fruit.
- **Foreign investors returned.** Warren Buffett's large stakes in Japan's five big trading houses lent a seal of approval.
- **Households began to invest.** An expanded tax-free savings scheme, **NISA**, from January 2024 encouraged households to move some of their vast cash deposits into shares.

The index kept going: to a record close of 72,366 on 25 June 2026, in a rally led by technology and AI-related stocks.

## The August 2024 crash

On 31 July 2024 the BOJ raised its rate to 0.25% and announced a plan to roughly halve its monthly bond purchases by early 2026. Two days later, weak American jobs data stoked fears of a US recession and rapid US rate cuts. With the rate gap suddenly expected to narrow from both ends, the yen surged and carry trades were unwound in a stampede.

On 5 August the Nikkei fell **12.4%**, its worst day since the crash of October 1987, before rebounding by about 10% the next day. On 7 August the BOJ's deputy governor, **Shinichi Uchida**, said the bank would not raise rates while markets were unstable, and calm returned.

The lesson was sobering. After decades as the world's source of cheap funding, Japan could no longer normalise policy without the rest of the world feeling it. Lesson 10 explains why.

---

## Politics shifts

Kishida stepped down in 2024 and **Shigeru Ishiba** became prime minister in October. He called a snap election and lost the ruling coalition's majority in the lower house, leaving a minority government that had to bargain with opposition parties for every budget. Their demands, such as raising the income level at which tax starts to bite, tilted policy towards relief for households squeezed by inflation.

---

## What it left behind

By the end of 2024 Japan had left the zero-rate world behind: core inflation had been at or above 2% for more than two and a half years, wages were rising at their fastest pace in a generation and stocks were at record highs. But real wages were still falling in most months, the yen was weak, and the BOJ had learned that every step towards normal carried global risk. The next phase would add a volatile new ingredient: politics.

## Check yourself

**1. On 21 October 2022 the Ministry of Finance spent about ¥5.6 trillion buying yen, when the rate was about 150 per dollar. Roughly how many dollars did it sell?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

5.6 trillion ÷ 150 ≈ **$37 billion**. To buy yen, Japan sells dollars from its foreign reserves, which is why interventions show up later as falls in Japan's holdings of US Treasuries (Lesson 10).

</details>

**2. A worker earning ¥300,000 a month gets the 2024 shunto average of 5.1%. About 1.5 to 2 points of that is automatic seniority pay. If inflation is 2.8%, is the pay scale itself rising faster than prices?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

The worker's pay rises by 300,000 × 0.051 = **¥15,300 a month**. But the **base-pay** part, the rise in the wage scale itself, is roughly 5.1% − 1.5 to 2 ≈ **3.1% to 3.6%**, which beats 2.8% inflation by a little. That base-pay rise is what the BOJ watches, because seniority pay does not raise the wage bill when older workers retire and younger ones replace them.

</details>

**3. The Nikkei closed at 35,910 on 2 August 2024 and 31,458 on 5 August, then rose 10.2% on 6 August. Where did it close on the 6th, and how much of the 5 August loss did that recover?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

31,458 × 1.102 ≈ **34,667** (the actual close was 34,675). The 5 August fall was 35,910 − 31,458 = 4,452 points; the rebound was about 3,217 points, roughly **72%** of it. Big percentage rises on smaller bases recover less than they look.

</details>

**4. Why did the yen weaken after the BOJ ended negative rates in March 2024?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because what drives the yen is the **gap** with US rates, and a rise of about 0.2 points barely dented a gap of more than 5 points. The BOJ also signalled that it would move slowly, so markets saw no reason to expect the gap to narrow soon. Expected rate paths matter as much as today's rate.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 7

- The Fed's 2022 hikes blew the **rate gap** wide open and the yen fell to about 152, then 162.
- Japan's inflation began as **imported** (energy, weak yen) but spread to **services** and **wages**.
- The **shunto** broke out: 3.6% in 2023 and 5.1% in 2024, giving the BOJ its proof.
- **March 2024** ended negative rates, YCC and stock buying; the yen still weakened.
- The **Nikkei** beat its 1989 peak in February 2024, on profits and governance rather than a bubble.
- **5 August 2024** showed the world's dependence on cheap yen: a small hike plus a US scare caused Tokyo's worst day since 1987.

Lesson 7 brings the story to today.

# Lesson 10: Why It Matters Beyond Japan

*Japan is one of the world's five largest economies, but its financial footprint is bigger still. Its exit from three decades of easy money now reaches the rest of the world through four channels.*

A Japanese life insurer that needs long-dated assets to match its policies can now earn about 4.1% on a 30-year JGB with no currency risk. A 30-year US Treasury pays more, about 5.6%. But an insurer cannot take the currency risk on its whole portfolio, so it **hedges**: it agrees today to sell its future dollars back for yen at a fixed rate. The cost of that hedge is roughly the gap between American and Japanese short-term interest rates, about 2.8 percentage points a year at present. After hedging, the Treasury yields only about 2.8%, well below the yen bond.

Even a modest shift of money back home would mean fewer buyers for Treasuries, gilts and euro-area bonds, pushing their yields up. That is a key reason the US Treasury joined the July 2026 yen intervention: a disorderly slide in the yen and in JGBs could spill straight into American borrowing costs.

## Channel 2: The carry trade

For years cheap yen loans funded leveraged bets on everything from Mexican pesos to American technology shares. Nobody knows the exact size of these positions, because they are spread across banks, hedge funds and derivatives. The arithmetic of a single trade shows why they are dangerous.

Borrow ¥100 million at 0.5%, convert it at 160 yen per dollar into $625,000, and invest it at 4% for a year. If the yen ends the year where it started, you repay ¥100.5 million and your $650,000 converts back to ¥104 million: a profit of ¥3.5 million, or 3.5% on money you never had. But the trade breaks even at about 154.6 yen per dollar. A rise in the yen of only about 3.5% wipes out a whole year of interest, and a 10% rise turns it into a loss of more than ¥6 million. With leverage, multiply all of that several times over.

That asymmetry explains August 2024. A modest BOJ hike and a weak US jobs report together made a stronger yen look likely. Thousands of carry traders rushed for the same exit at once, buying yen to repay their loans, which strengthened the yen further and forced more of them out. The result was Tokyo's worst day since 1987, with tremors across world markets. Every BOJ hike carries a small risk of a repeat, which is why the bank moves in quarter-point steps and signals each one carefully.

## Channel 3: Competition and capital

A weak yen makes Japanese cars, machinery and components cheaper abroad, intensifying the pressure on European and American manufacturers. It also makes Japanese hotels, property and companies look cheap to foreign buyers, and turns Japan into one of the world's great tourist bargains.

At the same time, cash-rich Japanese firms keep buying abroad to escape a shrinking home market, even though the weak yen makes foreign assets more expensive. **Nippon Steel's** acquisition of **U.S. Steel** in 2025, after a long political fight in Washington, is the best-known example.

---

## Channel 4: A preview of everyone's future

Europe and America are ageing, carry heavy public debts and have used Japan's toolkit since 2008. Japan's experience offers lessons that travel well.

1. **Exiting too early is costly.** The BOJ raised rates in 2000 and 2006 with inflation near zero and had to reverse both times.
2. **Fiscal and monetary policy must not fight each other.** In 2014 a tax rise cancelled much of the central bank's stimulus; in 2026 subsidies and tax cuts are pulling against rate rises.
3. **Unwinding quantitative easing takes years** and leaves the central bank exposed to losses, as it pays more on reserves than it earns on old bonds.
4. **Bond markets that seem permanently docile can wake up abruptly** when inflation returns and governments keep spending.

**1. Using the figures above (30-year Treasury 5.61%, US short rate 4.00%, Japanese short rate 1.23%), what is the hedged return on the Treasury, and how does it compare with a 30-year JGB at 4.15%?**

Hedge cost ≈ 4.00 − 1.23 = 2.77 points. Hedged return ≈ 5.61 − 2.77 = **2.84%**, about **1.3 points less** than the JGB. For a hedged Japanese investor, home now wins, which is what makes the "savings tide" a live risk for other bond markets.

**2. In the carry trade above, what is the profit or loss if the yen ends the year at 150?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

$625,000 × 1.04 = $650,000. At 150 that is ¥97.5 million. Repay ¥100.5 million. Result: **a loss of ¥3 million**, even though the dollar investment earned its full 4%.

</details>

**3. If Japan sells $50 billion of Treasuries to fund an intervention, why might US 10-year yields rise?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Selling pushes bond prices down, and lower prices mean higher yields. One sale of that size is absorbed easily in a market of more than $28 trillion, but a series of them, or the fear of them, adds to the supply other investors must take on, and they demand a slightly higher yield to do so. That is why Washington takes the yen's stability seriously.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 11

- Japan is a **creditor giant**: about ¥562 trillion of net foreign assets and about **$1.1 trillion** of US Treasuries.
- With JGB yields up and hedging expensive, a **hedged Japanese investor now earns more at home**; a shift home would lift yields abroad.
- The **carry trade** earns small, steady gains and suffers sudden, large losses; a yen rise of about **3.5%** can erase a year's carry.
- A weak yen sharpens **competition**, while Japanese firms keep **buying abroad**.
- Japan is a **preview** of the policy dilemmas facing every ageing, indebted economy.

# Lesson 1: The Machine

*Five dials, three channels and one number that matters more than the rest. Learn the wiring once and every era of Japan's story becomes easier to follow.*

## Five dials, wired together

Japan's economy can be read through five dials: **growth, inflation, interest rates, the yen and the bond market**. They are wired together, so turning one moves the others. A rate rise by the Bank of Japan (BOJ) changes what the yen is worth, what households pay on their mortgages, what the government pays on its debt and, eventually, how fast prices rise. Every era in this guide is a story about someone trying to turn one dial without breaking another.

Here is the whole machine on one page. The rest of this lesson takes it apart, one dial at a time.

Three channels connect the dials. The **currency channel** runs from the gap between Japanese and American interest rates to the yen, and from the yen to the price of imported fuel and food. The **demand channel** runs from borrowing costs to spending, hiring and, eventually, wages. The **bond channel** runs from government bond yields to the state's interest bill and to the balance sheets of the banks and insurers that own those bonds. The BOJ's real target sits where the channels meet: inflation of about 2% that is driven by rising wages and sustains itself, without the crutch of a weak yen.

## Dial 1: Growth, and why the nominal number matters so much

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** measures everything an economy produces. **Real GDP** strips out price changes and tracks volume: how many cars, haircuts and train journeys. **Nominal GDP** counts output in today's yen, so it rises with both volume and prices. In most countries the gap between the two is a technicality. In Japan it is the heart of the story.

From the mid-1990s to the mid-2010s Japan's nominal GDP went essentially nowhere, drifting between roughly ¥500 trillion and ¥550 trillion for two decades. Real output was inching up, but prices were gently falling, and the two cancelled out. That matters because almost everything people experience lives in nominal terms. A company's revenue, a worker's pay packet, the government's tax receipts and the size of a mortgage are all yen amounts. When nominal GDP is flat, revenues are flat, pay rises are rare and debts never shrink relative to incomes. A flat nominal economy feels like stagnation to everyone inside it, even when output per person is improving.

Look at the right-hand end of the chart. Nominal GDP passed ¥600 trillion in fiscal 2023 and was running at about ¥689 trillion a year by the middle of 2026. Real GDP grew far more slowly over the same years. The difference is inflation, which finally arrived after 2022, and that one fact explains much of what has changed since.

Japan's working-age population (people aged 15 to 64) peaked in the mid-1990s and has been falling ever since; the total population peaked at about 128 million around 2008 to 2010 and has been shrinking too. With fewer workers each year, Japan's sustainable or **potential** growth rate, the pace it can grow without overheating, is estimated at only around 0.5% a year.

That reframes the "lost decades". Measured per working-age person, Japan's record since 2000 looks far more respectable than its headline GDP suggests. The problem was less that each worker produced too little than that there were fewer workers every year. It also helps solve one of this guide's puzzles: why two decades of stagnation never brought mass unemployment. Unemployment peaked at about 5.5%, in 2002 and again in 2009, because a shrinking workforce kept jobs relatively plentiful even when growth was weak.

## Dial 2: Inflation, and why falling prices are a trap

**Deflation**, a sustained fall in the general level of prices, sounds like good news. Who objects to cheaper shopping? But deflation corrodes an economy in three ways.

- **People wait.** If you expect a television or a car to be cheaper next year, you put off buying it. Multiply that by a hundred million people and demand stalls.
- **Pay freezes.** Firms that cannot raise prices cannot raise wages, so incomes stagnate even when jobs are secure.
- **Debts grow heavier.** Debts are fixed in yen. When prices and wages fall, every loan takes a larger share of income to service.

### The deepest problem is arithmetic

What matters for borrowing and investment is not the interest rate you see quoted but the **real interest rate**: the nominal rate minus expected inflation. A business deciding whether to borrow ¥100 million for a new factory cares about what the loan costs in terms of the things it will sell.

> **Real interest rate = nominal interest rate − expected inflation**

A central bank cannot push nominal rates far below zero, because savers would simply hold cash. So if prices are falling by 1% a year, even a 0% policy rate is really a real rate of plus 1%. That is tight money, applied to an economy that needs stimulus. Deflation disarms the central bank at exactly the moment it is needed.

The right-hand panel is today. Even after the September 2026 rise to 1.25%, underlying inflation is running at about 1.9%, so the real rate is still about −0.7%. Policy is still pressing gently on the accelerator, not the brake. Economists estimate that Japan's **neutral rate**, the rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy, lies somewhere between about 1% and 2.5% in nominal terms, and much of today's argument about how far the BOJ should go is an argument about where in that range the true number sits.

### When zero becomes a habit

Over two decades, zero inflation hardened into a social norm. A firm that raised its prices risked losing customers to rivals that did not, so nobody moved first. In 2016 the makers of Garigari-kun, a popular ice lolly, raised its price from ¥60 to ¥70, its first increase in 25 years, and ran a television advert in which the company's executives bowed in apology. That is what entrenched deflation looks like: not a collapse, but a frozen price system.

Two terms will help later.

- Japan's headline inflation measure is **core CPI**, consumer prices excluding volatile fresh food. **Core-core CPI** also excludes energy, which makes it a better guide to underlying, home-grown price pressure.
- The BOJ distinguishes **"good" inflation**, driven by rising wages and domestic demand, from **"bad" inflation**, driven by a weak yen and costly imports, which squeezes households without any matching rise in pay. The whole of Japan's recent history turns on which kind it has.

## Dial 3: Interest rates, and what to do when the lever hits zero

The BOJ directly sets one short-term rate: the **overnight call rate**, at which banks lend to each other until the next day. Longer-term rates, such as the yield on 10-year **Japanese government bonds (JGBs)**, are normally set by markets. They reflect where investors expect short rates to go over the next ten years, plus a **term premium** for tying money up for longer. (The [bond guide](?slug=global-bond-markets&lesson=lesson-02-what-sets-the-level-of-yields) builds that idea from first principles.)

When the short rate reaches zero and the economy still needs help, a central bank must find other levers. There are four, and Japan used all of them.

| Tool | What it does | Japan's version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Forward guidance** | Promise to keep rates low for a long time, so long-term rates fall too | 1999: zero rates "until deflationary concerns are dispelled" |
| **Quantitative easing (QE)** | Create money to buy bonds, pushing long-term yields down | 2001 to 2006, then on a vast scale from 2013 |
| **Negative interest rates** | Charge banks for holding spare reserves at the central bank | −0.1% on a slice of reserves, 2016 to 2024 |
| **Yield curve control (YCC)** | Announce a target for a long-term yield and buy whatever it takes to hold it | 10-year yield pinned near zero, 2016 to 2024 |

Lessons 2 to 6 tell how each one was tried, and what it did.

The bottom row of the figure shows why exporters love a weak yen. The same $100 of sales abroad was worth about ¥7,600 to a Japanese company at the yen's strongest, in 2011, and about ¥16,400 at its weakest, in 2026: more than twice as many yen for exactly the same sale.

Four forces push the yen around.

1. **The interest rate gap.** This is the strongest force. When American rates sit far above Japanese ones, money flows out of yen and into dollars in search of yield.
2. **Risk appetite.** In a global panic, Japanese investors bring money home and speculators close their yen-funded bets, so the yen jumps even when the crisis has nothing to do with Japan. That is the yen's reputation as a **safe haven**.
3. **Trade, especially energy.** Japan imports almost all its oil and gas, and must sell yen to pay for it. When fuel prices soar, the yen tends to weaken.
4. **Structural flows.** Japanese companies investing abroad, households buying foreign shares and payments to foreign technology firms create a steady stream of yen selling.

The interest rate gap gives rise to the famous **carry trade**: borrow in yen at close to zero, invest in a higher-yielding currency and pocket the difference. It works beautifully while the yen is calm or weakening. When the yen jumps, everyone tries to get out at once, which is why yen rallies can be so violent. Lesson 10 works through the numbers.

A weak yen is a mixed blessing. It inflates the yen value of exporters' overseas profits, lifts the stock market and has made Japan a magnet for tourists. But it raises the cost of imported fuel, food and materials, and those costs land on households whose wages have not kept pace. One institutional detail matters: **currency intervention is decided by the Ministry of Finance (MOF), not the BOJ**, which simply carries out the trades.

## Dial 5: The bond market, and why a debt mountain did not cause a crisis

Japan's gross government debt is about twice the size of its economy, the highest ratio in the rich world. For decades investors predicted a crash, and betting against JGBs became known as the **"widowmaker" trade** because it ruined so many who tried it. Four features explain the calm.

1. **The debt is in yen and owed at home.** It is held overwhelmingly by Japanese banks, insurers, pension funds and, latterly, the BOJ itself, so there is little risk of foreign creditors fleeing.
2. **Japan saves more than it invests.** The country runs a surplus with the rest of the world, so the state never needed foreign money.
3. **Deflation made low yields rational.** A 0.5% yield is a decent real return when prices are falling.
4. **The central bank became the dominant buyer.** From 2013 the BOJ bought bonds on such a scale that it came to own about half of them.

### The arithmetic of debt

The arithmetic rests on one comparison: the interest rate paid on the debt (call it **r**) against the growth rate of nominal GDP (**g**). If nominal GDP grows faster than the interest bill, the debt ratio can fall even while the government runs a modest deficit, because the economy is outgrowing the debt.

> **Change in the debt ratio ≈ debt ratio × (r − g) ÷ (1 + g) + primary deficit**
>
> The **primary deficit** is the budget deficit before interest payments.

On the right, flip the numbers to 3% interest and 2% growth, and the same deficit makes the ratio *rise* by about 4 points a year. As yields climb, maturing bonds are refinanced at higher rates and the advantage narrows. That is the debate Lesson 9 takes up.

## The big idea to carry forward

For a quarter of a century, Japan's central problem was escaping a **deflationary equilibrium**: a self-reinforcing state in which nobody raised prices or wages because nobody expected anyone else to. Today the problem has flipped. The question is how to bring interest rates back to normal without breaking the currency, the bond market or the recovery itself.

## Check yourself

**1. The policy rate is 0.5% and inflation is running at 3%. What is the real interest rate, and is policy tight or loose?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Real rate = 0.5% − 3% = **−2.5%**. A negative real rate means borrowers repay less, in terms of what money buys, than they borrowed. Policy is **loose**: it is still encouraging borrowing and spending.

</details>

**2. A Japanese machine-tool maker sells a machine in America for $50,000. How many yen is the sale worth at 130 yen per dollar, and at 150? By what percentage does the yen value change?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

At 130: 50,000 × 130 = **¥6.5 million**. At 150: 50,000 × 150 = **¥7.5 million**. That is ¥1 million more, a rise of 1 ÷ 6.5 ≈ **15.4%**, without selling a single extra machine. A weaker yen (a higher number) flatters exporters' profits.

</details>

**3. Debt is 200% of GDP. The average interest rate on it is 1.5%, nominal GDP grows 3% and the primary deficit is 2.5% of GDP. Does the debt ratio rise or fall in a year, and by roughly how much?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Interest-growth effect: 200 × (0.015 − 0.03) ÷ 1.03 ≈ −2.9 points. Add the primary deficit of 2.5 points. The ratio changes by about **−0.4 points**, so it falls very slightly, to about 199.6%. Growth only just outruns the deficit. A little more interest or a little less growth and the ratio would rise.

</details>

**4. Why can't a central bank simply cut rates to −5% to fight deflation?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because cash pays 0%. If banks and savers were charged 5% a year to hold deposits, they would withdraw banknotes and keep them in a safe. That floor, a little below zero, is why deflation is so dangerous: with prices falling, even a zero rate is a positive real rate, and the central bank cannot cut further. Japan's negative rate never went below −0.1%, and Lesson 4 shows how even that backfired.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 2

- Japan has **five dials**: growth, inflation, interest rates, the yen and the bond market, connected by a **currency channel**, a **demand channel** and a **bond channel**.
- In Japan the **nominal** economy is the story: flat for twenty years, rising fast since 2022.
- The **real interest rate** decides whether policy is tight or loose. Deflation makes even a zero rate tight.
- The yen is quoted **backwards**: a bigger number is a weaker yen. Its strongest single driver is the **US-Japan interest rate gap**.
- A debt ratio falls when **nominal growth beats the interest rate**. Rising yields narrow that cushion over many years.

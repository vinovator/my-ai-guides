# Lesson 2: 1985 to 2005, the Bubble, the Bust and the Birth of Deflation

*How a deal to weaken the dollar led to the most famous asset bubble of the twentieth century, why its collapse took fifteen years to clean up, and how Japan became the first rich country in post-war history to slip into lasting deflation.*

---

## What was pressing

In 1985, a dollar that was too strong. By 1990, a bubble that had to be stopped. By 1998, a banking system on the brink. Everything Japan did over the next twenty years was shaped by this backdrop, so it is worth setting the stage before the main story begins.

---

## The bubble

In September 1985 the finance ministers of the five largest economies met at the Plaza Hotel in New York and agreed to push the dollar down. The **Plaza Accord** worked spectacularly. Within about two years the yen had roughly doubled against the dollar, from around 240 to around 120.

For Japanese exporters this was a shock: every dollar of overseas sales was suddenly worth half as many yen. To cushion them, the BOJ cut its discount rate (then its main policy rate) step by step, to a then-record low of **2.5%** in February 1987, and held it there for more than two years.

Cheap money met newly deregulated banks that were willing to lend freely against land, and asset prices took off. Companies borrowed against their land to buy shares, which rose, which let them borrow more. The Nikkei 225 stock index roughly tripled in four years, to an all-time closing peak of **38,916 on 29 December 1989**. A widely repeated estimate held that the grounds of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo were worth more than all the land in California.

### The bust

Alarmed by soaring land prices, the BOJ began raising rates in May 1989, and its new governor, **Yasushi Mieno**, who took over in December 1989, kept going. By August 1990 the discount rate was **6%**. In 1990 the Ministry of Finance also capped the growth of bank lending to property. The bubble did not deflate gently; it burst. Share prices halved within three years, and land prices began a decline that lasted roughly fifteen years.

### Why the bust lasted so long

The collapse left companies with assets worth far less than their debts. The economist Richard Koo called the result a **balance sheet recession**. A company that discovers it is technically insolvent stops trying to maximise profit and starts trying to minimise debt. It uses every yen of cash flow to pay down loans instead of investing, however low interest rates go. When a whole corporate sector does that at once, cheap money gains almost no traction: nobody wants to borrow.

Banks, meanwhile, hid their bad loans. Rather than admit losses that would wipe out their capital, they kept lending to insolvent borrowers so those borrowers could keep paying interest. These **zombie companies** survived on life support, crowded out healthier rivals, and dragged down productivity across whole industries such as construction and property.

## The choices of the 1990s

Governments responded with repeated packages of public works: roads, bridges and dams. They propped up demand but piled up debt.

Then came a decision Japan still argues about. In April 1997, believing the recovery was secure, the government of **Ryutaro Hashimoto** raised the consumption tax, a sales tax, from 3% to 5% and cut spending. Within months the Asian financial crisis struck. In November 1997 the brokerage **Yamaichi Securities** and **Hokkaido Takushoku Bank**, one of the country's big city banks, collapsed. Japan slid into recession. In 1998 two of the major long-term credit banks were nationalised and the state injected public money into the banking system to keep it standing.

By the turn of the century prices were falling year after year. Deflation had arrived.

Strip those out and the trend is a slow slide from about 3% at the height of the bubble to below zero by 1999, where it stayed for most of the next fourteen years.

## The world's first unconventional central bank

In April 1998 a new law made the BOJ independent of the Ministry of Finance. Its new freedom arrived just as conventional policy ran out of room.

- **February 1999: zero rates.** The BOJ cut its overnight rate to effectively zero, and in April promised to keep it there "until deflationary concerns are dispelled". This was an early form of **forward guidance**, and Japan was the first major economy to reach the zero floor.
- **August 2000: a premature exit.** Judging that the economy had turned, the BOJ raised its rate to 0.25%, voting down a government request to delay. The global dot-com bubble was bursting at the same moment. The decision is still cited in textbooks as a classic example of tightening too soon.
- **March 2001: the world's first quantitative easing.** The BOJ reversed course and began targeting the *quantity* of money that banks held in their accounts at the central bank, rather than the *price* of money. It would buy government bonds until those balances reached its target, flooding the banking system with reserves.

## Cleaning up

**Junichiro Koizumi**, prime minister from 2001 to 2006, promised "no reform, no growth". From 2002 his economic and financial services minister, **Heizo Takenaka**, forced banks to value their bad loans honestly and to raise capital or face state control. The major banks' bad-loan ratio was cut by more than half by 2005, from above 8% to below 3%.

At the same time the Ministry of Finance fought the yen. Between January 2003 and March 2004 it sold a record **¥35 trillion** to stop the yen rising, a reminder that for most of this period Japan's currency problem was a yen that was *too strong*. (Lesson 7's history of intervention shows how completely that has reversed.) The Nikkei bottomed at **7,608 on 28 April 2003**, about a fifth of its 1989 peak, and then recovered as exports to a booming China and a debt-fuelled America took off.

---

## What it left behind

Three legacies shaped everything that followed.

1. **A deflationary mindset.** Firms stopped raising prices and workers stopped expecting pay rises. Prices that do not move become a habit that is hard to break.
2. **A defensive corporate sector.** Companies hoarded cash, cut investment and replaced permanent staff with cheaper part-time and temporary workers. Such **non-regular** workers made up about a fifth of employees around 1990 and about a third by the mid-2000s, and they are paid less, trained less and rarely unionised.
3. **A heavily indebted state, and a central bank with a reputation for tightening too soon.** Fairly or not, the BOJ was now seen as the institution that would always leave the party early.

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because the constraint is not the price of credit but the appetite for it. ## What to carry into Lesson 3

- The **Plaza Accord** doubled the yen; cheap money to cushion exporters fed a **land and stock bubble**.
- The bust created a **balance sheet recession** and **zombie** firms and banks, which is why it lasted so long.
- A premature **tax rise** in 1997 and a banking crisis tipped Japan into **deflation** by 1999.
- The BOJ invented **zero rates**, **forward guidance** and **quantitative easing**, but also made a textbook **premature exit** in 2000.
- For most of this era the problem was a yen that was **too strong**.

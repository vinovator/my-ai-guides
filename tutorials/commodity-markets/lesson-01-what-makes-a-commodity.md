# Lesson 1: What Makes Something a Commodity

*Why a gold bar is a commodity and a painting is not, the four families of commodities, and why governments treat them as matters of national security.*

---

## Sameness is the whole point

Picture two tonnes of copper sitting side by side in a warehouse in Rotterdam. One was mined in Chile and refined in China; the other came from a mine in Zambia and a smelter in Poland. If both meet the London Metal Exchange's specification for **Grade A copper cathode**, and both come from brands the exchange has approved, then nobody who buys them cares which is which. They are worth exactly the same.

That indifference is what makes something a **commodity**: a raw or lightly processed material that is standardised enough to be traded without anyone inspecting each unit. A barrel of crude oil of a defined grade, a bushel of wheat, a tonne of copper and a bar of gold all qualify, because one unit is as good as another. A branded smartphone, a painting or a house does not. Each of those is different, so each has to be inspected, haggled over and priced on its own.

Economists call this property **fungibility**, and almost everything in the rest of this guide flows from it. Because units are interchangeable, strangers on different continents can trade them without meeting. Because they can trade without meeting, they can trade on standard contracts. And because thousands of people trade the same standard contract, a single benchmark price emerges that can guide billions of dollars of physical deals around the world.

"Lightly processed" matters too. Diesel, steel, refined sugar and soybean meal have all been through a factory, but they are still made to standard specifications and still trade as commodities. What disqualifies something is not processing; it is individuality.

## Standardisation is the secret

Sameness does not happen by accident. Exchanges and industry bodies publish precise specifications so that a buyer knows exactly what they are getting without looking.

The cleanest example is gold. The London Bullion Market Association, which sets the standards for the world's largest over-the-counter gold market, defines a **London Good Delivery bar**.

Any bar that meets the specification is accepted by every participant in the London market, which is what lets gold change hands between banks on opposite sides of the world without anyone opening a vault door. The specification also explains a unit that puzzles newcomers. Precious metals are weighed in **troy ounces**, and a troy ounce is 31.1035 grams, about 10% heavier than the ordinary ounce used for flour. So a 400-ounce bar weighs 400 × 31.1035 = 12,441 grams, or about 12.4 kilograms: roughly the weight of a car tyre, in a bar small enough to hold in two hands.

Copper works the same way. The London Metal Exchange specifies the exact chemical grade of copper it will accept and keeps a list of approved producers' brands. Crude oil contracts specify density and sulphur content; wheat contracts specify the class of wheat, its grade and a minimum test weight, a measure of how dense the grain is.

### Price, place and time

Once the product is defined, very little is left to negotiate. The quality is fixed by the specification, so the bargaining comes down to three things: **price, place and time of delivery**.

That is why the same commodity carries slightly different prices in different places. A barrel of crude in Texas, a barrel in the North Sea and a barrel landed at an Indian refinery are all "oil", but they differ in quality (some crudes are lighter or lower in sulphur, and yield more petrol and diesel), in the cost of transporting them to where they are needed, in taxes and in the currency the buyer pays with. Each difference shows up as a gap between prices. Lesson 3 shows how the industry handles this: physical deals are priced as a benchmark plus or minus a differential for exactly these factors.

> A commodity is defined by its specification, not its origin. Once the grade is fixed, a market only has to agree on three things: how much, where, and when.

Two of those benchmark names will come up constantly. **Brent** is the price of crude oil loaded in the North Sea, and it prices most of the oil traded internationally. **WTI** (West Texas Intermediate) is the American benchmark, priced for delivery at a pipeline and storage hub in Cushing, Oklahoma. The fourth family is the odd one out: nothing is mined or grown. A carbon allowance is a permit to emit one tonne of carbon dioxide, and it exists only because a government capped emissions and let the permits trade.

When commodity prices move, the effect ripples outward into inflation, interest rates, company profits, government budgets and household bills. Lesson 8 follows each of those channels in turn.

### The scale is enormous

The world burns more than 100 million barrels of oil a day. A barrel is 42 US gallons, or about 159 litres, so that is roughly 16 billion litres every day, which would fill some six thousand Olympic swimming pools. Many countries rely on a handful of commodities for most of their export earnings: oil makes up most of Saudi Arabia's exports, and copper about half of Chile's.

For a large importer the dependence runs the other way. India imports nearly nine tenths of the crude oil it uses, and in the three months from April to June 2026 its crude import bill reached a record $49.66 billion. That single number shapes the rupee, inflation and the government's finances, as Lessons 8 and 9 explain.

### Commodities are strategic

Governments treat secure supplies of energy, food and critical minerals as matters of national security, and they act accordingly. They hold emergency stockpiles: members of the International Energy Agency must keep oil stocks equal to at least 90 days of their net imports. They ban exports to protect their own consumers, as India has repeatedly done with wheat, rice, sugar and onions. And they use sanctions and export controls as weapons, as the West did with Russian oil after 2022 and China did with rare earths in 2025.

> Learning commodities is, in effect, learning how the physical world is priced, and how power is exercised through it.

## Check yourself

**1. A barrel of West Texas crude and a barrel of North Sea crude are both "oil". Why do they trade at different prices?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because a commodity's price depends on place and time as well as on what it is. The two crudes differ in quality (density and sulphur content change how much petrol and diesel a refinery can get from them), in where they are and what it costs to move them to a buyer, and in the taxes and currency involved. Each of those differences appears as a gap between the two prices.

</details>

**2. A central bank holds 1,000 Good Delivery bars of 400 troy ounces each. How much gold is that in tonnes, and what is it worth at $4,300 an ounce?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

1,000 × 400 = 400,000 troy ounces. At 31.1035 grams per troy ounce that is 12,441,400 grams, or about **12.4 tonnes**. At $4,300 an ounce it is worth 400,000 × $4,300 = **$1.72 billion**.

</details>

**3. A smelter offers copper that is chemically identical to Grade A cathode but comes from a brand the London Metal Exchange has not approved. Why might it sell at a discount?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because sameness has to be certified to be useful. Metal from an unapproved brand cannot be delivered against an exchange contract, so buyers who rely on that guarantee would have to test it themselves, and fewer of them will want it. Less certainty and fewer buyers mean a lower price, even if the copper is just as good.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 2

- A commodity is a material standardised enough to trade **without inspecting each unit**. Interchangeability is what makes standard contracts and global benchmark prices possible.
- Specifications (the Good Delivery bar, Grade A copper) create that sameness. Once quality is fixed, what is left to negotiate is **price, place and time**.
- Four families: **energy, metals, agriculture**, and the policy-created **environmental** family. Each has its own drivers and benchmarks.
- Commodities are the first link in every supply chain, enormous in scale, and **strategic**: governments stockpile them, ban their export and use them as weapons.

Lesson 1 described what is traded. Lesson 2 introduces the people who trade it, and why each of them wants something different.

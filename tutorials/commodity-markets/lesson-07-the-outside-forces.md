# Lesson 7: The Outside Forces

*Benchmarks and the agencies that assess them, the dollar and the rupee, interest rates and gold's broken relationship with real yields, investor positioning, supercycles, the seasons, chokepoints and sanctions, and the ways an investor can take part.*

---

## Prices are pushed around from outside

Lessons 5 and 6 explained the forces inside a commodity market: supply, demand, stocks and the forward curve. But a copper price is also moved by things that have nothing to do with copper. A rate rise in Washington, a weak monsoon over Kerala, a drone attack in the Red Sea, a pension fund rebalancing its portfolio: each can move prices on a day when not a single mine changes its output. This lesson works through the outside forces professionals watch, from the most mechanical to the most political.

---

## Benchmarks and the people who assess them

A handful of benchmark prices act as reference points for whole industries, which is why so much attention goes into how they are set.

| Benchmark | What it prices | Where it is set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dated Brent** | Most of the world's internationally traded crude | Assessed from physical cargoes of North Sea crude and, since 2023, US WTI Midland delivered to Europe |
| **WTI** | US crude | Futures for delivery at Cushing, Oklahoma |
| **Dubai and Oman** | Middle Eastern crude sold to Asia | Assessed in Singapore and traded in Dubai |
| **Henry Hub, TTF, NBP** | Natural gas in the US, continental Europe and Britain | Futures on CME (Henry Hub) and ICE (TTF, NBP) |
| **LBMA Gold Price** | Gold worldwide | An electronic auction held twice a day in London |
| **LME official prices** | Copper, aluminium, zinc, nickel, lead and tin | The London Metal Exchange's daily trading sessions |

Where no exchange exists, **price reporting agencies** such as S&P Global Platts, Argus and Fastmarkets assess prices from the deals, bids and offers they observe. Because so much money rides on these numbers, their integrity is policed closely, and the manipulation scandals described in Lesson 10 led to independent administrators, transparent auctions and international principles for how assessments are made.

## The US dollar, and the rupee

Most commodities are priced in dollars. When the dollar strengthens, commodities become more expensive in other currencies, which tends to dampen demand and push dollar prices down. A weaker dollar tends to do the opposite. Treat this as a tendency, not a law: a big enough supply shock overwhelms it.

For a buyer in Britain or India, the exchange rate matters as much as the dollar price. India pays for its oil in dollars, so when the rupee weakens, India's oil bill rises even if the world price does not move. That is why prices on India's MCX exchange sometimes rise on days when international prices do not.

The rupee weakened from about 71 to the dollar in January 2020 to about 95 in September 2026, and the effect compounds with the oil price itself. Measured in dollars, Brent in September 2026 was 84% above its January 2020 level; measured in rupees, it was 146% above.

Japan lived the same arithmetic in 2026, with the yen near its weakest against the dollar in about forty years. A weak currency turns every oil shock into a bigger one at home, which is why Tokyo intervened to support the yen with record sums; the [Japan guide](?slug=japan-economy&lesson=lesson-01-the-machine) explains what drives the yen.

## Interest rates and inflation

Interest rates reach commodities through three doors.

1. **The cost of carry.** Higher rates make it more expensive to finance inventory, which steepens contango and encourages holders to run stocks down (Lesson 6).
2. **Demand.** Higher rates slow economic growth, and with it the demand for energy and metals.
3. **The opportunity cost of gold.** Gold pays no interest. When safe assets pay more, holding gold costs more in income forgone.

The third door produced one of the most reliable relationships in finance. For decades, gold moved inversely with **real interest rates**, meaning rates after inflation, usually measured by the yield on US inflation-protected government bonds. (The [bond guide](?slug=global-bond-markets&lesson=lesson-02-what-sets-the-level-of-yields) explains real yields from first principles.)

From 2006 to 2021 the blue points slope neatly downward: gold was dearest when real rates were lowest, and it peaked around $1,800 when they were negative. Then the relationship broke. Real yields jumped to around 2%, a level that history said should have crushed gold, and gold instead more than doubled. The reason was a buyer that does not care about yield. After Western sanctions froze much of Russia's central bank reserves in 2022, central banks around the world bought more than 1,000 tonnes of gold a year for three years running, more than double their average pace of the previous decade.

> Relationships in commodity markets are **tendencies that hold until a bigger force takes over**. The model was not wrong for twenty years; it simply stopped describing who was buying.

Commodities also feed inflation directly, through petrol, heating bills and food, which is why central banks watch them so closely. Lesson 8 follows that channel.

## Money flows and positioning

Financial investors can move prices in the short run, especially when many of them are positioned the same way. Every Friday, the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission publishes the **Commitments of Traders** report, showing how producers and merchants, swap dealers and "managed money" funds were positioned as of the previous Tuesday. When funds are heavily long and the news turns, their rush for the exit can trigger sharp falls; a crowded short can trigger sharp rises.

Whether financial money distorts prices in the long run is still debated. When oil hit $147 in 2008, index investors were widely blamed. Most academic studies found that supply and demand did most of the work, and the honest summary is that **flows can exaggerate moves without usually creating them**.

## Cycles and supercycles

Lesson 5 described the investment cycle: high prices trigger investment, new supply arrives years later, often all at once, and prices collapse; low prices starve investment, and shortages appear years later. Because mines and oilfields take so long to build, a single turn can last a decade.

A **supercycle** is a much longer upswing, lasting a decade or more, driven by structural demand: post-war reconstruction, or China's industrialisation in the 2000s, when a country of more than a billion people built cities, railways and factories at a pace the world had never seen.

Two things stand out. Energy prices, adjusted for inflation, spent most of the 1980s and 1990s at a fraction of their 1970s peak, which is how long the cure for high prices can take to wear off. And metals and energy rose together in the 2000s because the same Chinese building boom pulled on both. Today analysts debate whether electrification, data centres and defence spending will drive a new supercycle in copper, aluminium and critical minerals. Copper's monthly average passed $14,000 a tonne in August and September 2026, the highest in dollar terms on record.

## Weather, climate and the seasons

Demand follows the seasons: gas and heating oil peak in winter, US petrol demand peaks in the summer driving season, and power demand jumps in heatwaves. Crops follow planting and harvest calendars in both hemispheres, so a frost in Brazil or a drought in the US Midwest can move world prices.

In India, the June to September southwest monsoon delivers roughly three quarters of the year's rainfall. It shapes food prices and rural incomes, and through rural incomes even the demand for gold, motorcycles and fertiliser. The **El Niño** weather pattern tends to weaken the monsoon and bring drought to parts of Asia and Australia; its opposite, **La Niña**, tends to do the reverse. Climate change is adding volatility through heatwaves, floods and low river levels that disrupt shipping: low water on the Rhine in 2022 left barges carrying a fraction of their usual loads, and a drought in 2023 forced the Panama Canal to restrict the number of ships passing through it.

## Geopolitics, cartels, sanctions and export controls

**Spare capacity** is the oil market's emergency buffer: production that can be switched on within weeks. Most of it is held by OPEC+ members, chiefly Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. Remember where those countries are; Lesson 11 explains why it mattered in 2026.

**Physical chokepoints** matter enormously, because a large share of the world's oil squeezes through a few narrow passages.

The figure records a disruption that happened without a single oilfield being touched. After attacks on Red Sea shipping began in late 2023, flows through Bab el-Mandeb, the southern entrance to the Red Sea, fell by more than half, and tankers took the long way around Africa instead. Longer voyages need more ships, more fuel and more days at sea, so freight costs rose and more oil was tied up in transit.

**Sanctions** on Russia, Iran and Venezuela reshape trade routes and create discounted "shadow" markets served by ageing tankers with opaque ownership. **Export controls and bans** have become routine tools. China restricts exports of critical minerals, Indonesia banned exports of raw nickel ore in 2020 to force processing at home, and India has repeatedly restricted exports of wheat, rice, sugar and onions to protect its own consumers. Each of these moves prices by deciding where a commodity is allowed to go.

## Ways to get exposure

| Route | How it works | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Physical metal** | Bars, coins, jewellery | Storage, insurance, dealer spreads, and making charges on jewellery |
| **Futures and options** | Direct exposure on an exchange such as CME, ICE, LME or MCX | Leverage, margin calls, rolling costs |
| **ETFs and ETCs** | Funds holding metal (gold) or futures (oil) | Roll yield on futures-based funds; fees |
| **Producer shares** | Miners, oil companies, agribusiness | Company risks layered on top of the commodity |
| **Commodity indices** | Baskets such as the S&P GSCI or the Bloomberg Commodity Index | Weightings: the GSCI is heavily tilted towards energy |
| **CFDs and spread bets** (UK) | Leveraged bets on price moves | Most retail accounts lose money |

Every route carries some version of the ideas in Lessons 3 to 6: leverage and margin on futures, roll yield on futures-based funds, the cost of carry on physical metal.

## Check yourself

**1. Brent is flat at $90 a barrel for a year while the rupee weakens from 88 to 96 per dollar. What happens to the cost of a barrel in rupees?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

It rises from 90 × 88 = ₹7,920 to 90 × 96 = ₹8,640, an increase of **9.1%**, with no change at all in the world oil price.

</details>

**2. Real interest rates rose from below zero to about 2% between 2021 and 2023. The old relationship said gold should fall. Why did it rise instead?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because a new buyer arrived that does not care about yield. After sanctions froze much of Russia's reserves in 2022, central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes a year for three years, seeking an asset that no other government can freeze. Relationships hold until a bigger force takes over.

</details>

**3. Oil flowing through Bab el-Mandeb fell from 9.3 to 4.2 million barrels a day while flows around the Cape of Good Hope rose from 6.2 to 9.1. No oilfield lost output. Why did the disruption still raise costs?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because the oil had to travel further. Sailing around Africa instead of through the Red Sea adds days or weeks to a voyage, so the same daily flow needs more tankers, more fuel and more time at sea. Freight rates rise, insurance rises, and more oil is tied up in transit at any moment, which acts like a drain on usable stocks.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 8

- **Benchmarks** price whole industries, and the agencies that assess them are closely policed.
- **The dollar** tends to move against commodity prices, and for importers like India the **exchange rate** can matter as much as the world price.
- **Interest rates** act through the cost of carry, demand and the opportunity cost of gold, but **relationships are tendencies**: gold broke from real yields when central banks became its dominant buyer.
- **Flows** exaggerate moves more than they create them; **supercycles** come from structural demand; **seasons and weather** shape prices on a calendar.
- **Chokepoints, sanctions and export controls** decide where commodities may go, and so what they cost.

Every force so far moves a commodity price. Lesson 8 asks what a commodity price, once moved, does to everything else.

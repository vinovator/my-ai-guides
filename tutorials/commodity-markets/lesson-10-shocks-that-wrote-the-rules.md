# Lesson 10: Shocks That Wrote the Rules, 1973 to 2016

*Thirteen episodes, each a case study in one or two concepts from Lessons 1 to 9. Together they explain most of the institutions, rules and instincts that shape commodity markets today.*

---

## Why episodes rather than theory

Inelastic demand is a definition until the price of oil quadruples in a few months. A corner is a word until two Texan brothers try to buy most of the world's available silver. Almost every rule in today's commodity markets, from strategic oil reserves to position limits to the way the gold price is set, exists because something like this happened first.

Read each episode as a story: what happened, why, and what the market learned. Lesson 11 continues the story from 2018 to the present.

## 1. 1973 and 1979: The oil shocks

**Concepts:** inelastic demand, spare capacity, stagflation

In October 1973, during the Yom Kippur War, Arab oil producers cut their output and embargoed the United States and other countries that supported Israel. Within months the price of oil roughly quadrupled. In 1979 the Iranian revolution, followed in 1980 by the outbreak of the Iran-Iraq war, more than doubled it again.

Both shocks hit hard for the reason Lesson 5 gave: in the short run, demand for oil barely responds to price, so a modest loss of supply needs an enormous price rise to clear the market. And whoever controls the spare capacity, the oil that can be switched on or off at will, holds enormous power. Western economies suffered **stagflation**: high inflation and recession at the same time.

The shocks created institutions the world still relies on. The International Energy Agency was founded in 1974, and its members committed to hold emergency stocks; the US authorised its Strategic Petroleum Reserve the following year. The shocks also pushed oil away from prices posted by producers towards prices discovered on exchanges: NYMEX launched crude oil futures in 1983 and London launched Brent futures in 1988.

> **The lesson:** Supply shocks bite hardest where demand cannot respond, and control of spare capacity is market power. The institutions of today's oil market (emergency stocks, futures, the IEA) were built in response to these two crises.

Then the authorities moved. COMEX sharply raised margins and imposed limits on how many contracts anyone could hold, and in January 1980 it allowed only trades that closed positions. In March the Federal Reserve discouraged banks from lending for speculation. The price collapsed, and on **27 March 1980**, "Silver Thursday", the Hunts failed to meet a margin call. Silver fell to around $11 that day.

This is the classic **corner**. Control enough of the commodity available for delivery, and everyone who sold futures cannot find metal to deliver, so they must buy their contracts back at almost any price. The episode shows how margin rules and position limits defuse squeezes, how leverage turns a winning position into ruin, and that exchanges can and do change the rules in the middle of the game.

> **The lesson:** A corner works only until the exchange decides it will not. Leverage that builds a fortune in a rising market liquidates it in a falling one, and margin calls do not wait.

## 4. 1996: Sumitomo and "Mr Copper"

**Concepts:** concentrated positions, internal controls

Yasuo Hamanaka, the head copper trader at Japan's Sumitomo Corporation, used huge positions on the LME for years to hold copper prices up. When his activities were exposed in 1996, prices slumped, and Sumitomo eventually reported losses of about **$2.6 billion**.

Concentrated positions can distort prices, especially in markets dominated by a few large players, and weak internal controls let a single trader hide catastrophic risk for years. The affair led to tighter LME surveillance and rules on dominant positions.

> **The lesson:** The largest risk in a trading firm is often a trader nobody is watching. Concentration in a thin market can move the price, until the day it cannot.

## 5. 2001: Enron and the California power crisis

**Concepts:** opaque markets, credit risk, manipulation, electricity's peculiarity

Enron transformed itself from a pipeline company into the world's largest energy trader, running an online platform in lightly regulated over-the-counter markets. During the California electricity crisis of 2000 and 2001, which brought blackouts and soaring bills, its traders were later shown to have gamed supply and prices. Enron collapsed into bankruptcy in December 2001 amid accounting fraud.

Opaque, uncleared markets concentrate credit risk and invite manipulation, and electricity is especially vulnerable because it cannot be stored and supply must match demand second by second. The fallout strengthened the US energy regulator's powers against manipulation and foreshadowed the push, after 2008, to clear and report derivatives.

> **The lesson:** When nobody can see the positions, nobody can see the risk. Markets that cannot store their commodity are the easiest to game.

**Concepts:** export bans, the energy-food link, stocks

## 8. 2010: Dodd-Frank and the retreat of the banks

**Concepts:** clearing, regulation, who carries risk

After the financial crisis, the US Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 and Europe's EMIR rules pushed standardised over-the-counter derivatives into central clearing, required trade reporting and mandated position limits, while the Volcker Rule curbed banks' proprietary trading. Higher capital charges followed, along with scrutiny of banks that owned warehouses, tankers and power plants. JPMorgan sold its physical commodities business to the trader Mercuria in 2014, and Morgan Stanley sold its oil trading arm in 2015.

Regulation decides who carries risk. Commodity risk migrated from banks to independent trading houses and hedge funds, which grew more powerful but are less transparent. And central clearing, which reduces the risk that one counterparty's failure topples another, concentrates risk in clearing houses and produces enormous margin calls when markets turn violent, as Lesson 4 showed for 2022.

> **The lesson:** Rules do not remove risk; they move it. Follow where it went.

After US Senate hearings and lawsuits, the LME reformed its warehouse rules from 2014.

## 10. 2012: India's guar boom and bust

**Concepts:** global demand meets a local market, substitution, speculation

Guar gum, made from a bean grown mainly in Rajasthan, thickens the fluid used in fracking. As the US shale boom took off, demand exploded, and guar seed prices on India's NCDEX exchange rose roughly tenfold in about a year. The Forward Markets Commission suspended guar futures in March 2012 amid concerns about manipulation, and prices later collapsed as American users found substitutes.

Global demand can transmit with startling force into a local farm market. Speculative excess, squeezes and regulatory suspensions are recurring features of Indian farm futures, as the freeze described in Lesson 9 shows. And the episode is a lesson in substitution: when something becomes too expensive, buyers eventually find an alternative.

> **The lesson:** The cure for high prices includes substitutes nobody had bothered to look for while the commodity was cheap.

In effect these were financing deals promising fixed returns, and much of the stock supposedly sitting in warehouses did not exist. After the government halted new contracts in July 2013, NSEL's trading members defaulted on about **₹5,600 crore** (roughly $900 million at the time) owed to some 13,000 investors.

Regulatory gaps invite trouble, and NSEL sat outside full oversight by the commodity regulator. Warehouse receipts are only as good as the inspection behind them, and promised "risk-free" returns on commodities are a warning sign. The scandal led directly to the Forward Markets Commission's merger into SEBI in 2015.

> **The lesson:** If a commodity "trade" pays a fixed return whatever the price does, it is a loan, and you should ask who the borrower is and what secures it.

## 12. 2014: The London gold fix scandal

**Concepts:** benchmarks, conflicts of interest

For nearly a century, the London gold price was "fixed" in a private call among a handful of banks. In May 2014, the UK's Financial Conduct Authority fined Barclays £26 million after one of its traders manipulated the fix in 2012 to avoid paying out to a client. In March 2015 the old fix was replaced by the **LBMA Gold Price**, an electronic auction run by an independent administrator, and the silver fix was replaced as well.

Benchmarks underpin enormous numbers of contracts, so conflicts of interest in setting them are dangerous. The response (transparent auctions, independent administrators and benchmark regulation) mirrors the reforms that followed the LIBOR interest-rate scandal.

> **The lesson:** Whoever sets a benchmark must have no stake in where it lands, and everyone should be able to see how it was set.

The US lifted its ban on crude exports in December 2015. In December 2016, OPEC and a group of non-OPEC producers led by Russia agreed to cut output together, creating **OPEC+**.

Shale is "short-cycle" supply: a well can be drilled and producing within months, not the years a conventional field takes. That changed how quickly the cure for high prices works, and it limits a cartel's power, because higher-cost rivals can grow whenever the cartel props the price up. The deep contango of 2015 and 2016 made it profitable to store oil in tankers at sea, a textbook cost-of-carry trade (Lesson 6).

> **The lesson:** A cartel can push the price up only as far as its rivals let it. When rivals can respond in months, that is not very far.

## Check yourself

**1. A trader holds futures entitling them to take delivery of 10 million ounces of silver, but only 6 million ounces are available in exchange warehouses. What happens to traders who sold those futures as expiry approaches, and what can the exchange do?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

The sellers cannot all find metal to deliver, so they must buy their contracts back from the trader at whatever price is demanded: a **corner**. The exchange can raise margins, cap how many contracts anyone may hold, or allow only trades that close positions, which is exactly what COMEX did to the Hunts in 1980.

</details>

**2. India restricted rice exports in 2008 to protect its own consumers. Why did that push up the world price?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

It removed supply from the world market at a time when stocks were already thin, and importers panicked and bought ahead. Other exporters followed with restrictions of their own, so a tight market became a scramble. The shortage was not removed; it was exported.

</details>

**3. On the World Bank's monthly averages, oil went from $2.70 in September 1973 to $13.00 in January 1974, and silver from $6.20 in January 1979 to $38.90 in January 1980. By how many times did each rise?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Oil: 13.00 ÷ 2.70 ≈ **4.8 times** in four months. Silver: 38.90 ÷ 6.20 ≈ **6.3 times** in a year, and more than eight times at the daily peak near $50.

</details>

**4. Why did US shale weaken OPEC's ability to hold prices up?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because shale is short-cycle supply. When OPEC holds prices high, shale producers can drill and add output within months, taking market share. The cartel either accepts lower prices or keeps losing customers, which is the dilemma it resolved in 2014 by refusing to cut, and in 2016 by recruiting Russia and others into OPEC+.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 11

- The **oil shocks** built the IEA, strategic reserves and oil futures.
- **Corners and squeezes** (the Hunts, Sumitomo) are defused by margins, position limits and an exchange willing to change the rules.
- **Price support** (tin), **opacity** (Enron), **weak delivery rules** (aluminium) and **regulatory gaps** (NSEL) each produced a reform.
- **Supercycles** come from structural demand; **export bans** turn a tight market into a panic; **regulation** moves risk rather than removing it.
- **Short-cycle shale** limited OPEC's power and created OPEC+.

Lesson 11 continues the story into the present, where several of these lessons are being learned again.

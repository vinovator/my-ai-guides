# Lesson 2: Who Trades, and Why

*The six kinds of participant, what each of them wants, which side of the market each naturally takes, and why the price is simply where their needs meet.*

---

## Every price is a truce

A commodity price is not set by anyone. It is where the competing needs of very different people happen to meet on a given day. A copper miner wants a high price and a cable maker wants a low one. A grain merchant wants the price in one port to differ from the price in another, because that gap is how it earns a living. A hedge fund wants the price to move, in either direction, as long as it guessed right. A government wants the price stable and, above all, wants the stuff to keep arriving.

Six kinds of participant make the commodity world work, and each wants something different.

## 1. Producers: the natural sellers

Producers are the miners, oil and gas companies and farmers who take commodities out of the ground or grow them. Their costs are largely fixed long before the product is sold: a mine is dug, a well drilled or a crop planted months or years ahead. What they fear is a price collapse between spending the money and selling the output. So they want high prices and, above all, **predictable** ones.

That makes producers natural sellers in the futures market (Lesson 3 explains how futures work). Selling futures lets a producer lock in a price for output it has not yet produced. Because it involves selling, this is called a **short hedge**.

The names range from global miners such as BHP, Rio Tinto, Vale and Freeport-McMoRan to oil majors such as ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell and BP. National oil companies such as Saudi Aramco control most of the world's oil reserves. In India, the producer side runs from ONGC and Coal India to Vedanta, Hindalco and NMDC, and to well over a hundred million farm holdings, most of them smaller than two hectares.

## 2. Consumers and processors: the natural buyers

Consumers are the businesses that turn raw materials into something else. Refiners make fuel from crude, utilities burn gas to make power, steelmakers use iron ore and coal, airlines burn jet fuel, chocolate makers buy cocoa and jewellers buy gold. They fear the opposite of what producers fear: a price spike that wipes out their margin. So they want low and predictable costs, which makes them natural buyers of futures, a **long hedge**.

In India, Indian Oil and Reliance Industries, whose Jamnagar complex is the world's largest refining site, are huge buyers of crude, and Tata Steel and JSW Steel buy iron ore and coking coal. In the UK, think of airlines, utilities such as Centrica, and food manufacturers. In the US, the list runs from the airlines to food companies such as Hershey and Mondelez.

## 3. Merchants: the middlemen who make the physical system flow

Between the producer and the consumer sits a group most people have never heard of. Physical commodity traders buy from producers, then store, blend, ship and finance the goods before selling them to consumers. The largest energy traders are Vitol, Trafigura, Mercuria and Gunvor, and Glencore is both a miner and a trader. In agriculture the "ABCD" houses (ADM, Bunge, Cargill and Louis Dreyfus) are joined by China's COFCO and Singapore's Olam and Wilmar. Most are based in Geneva, Singapore, London or Houston, and many are privately owned, which keeps them out of the spotlight despite their size.

The crucial point about merchants is how they make money. It is mostly **not** by betting on whether prices go up. They earn by moving commodities:

- **across space**: buying where a commodity is cheap and selling where it is dear;
- **across time**: buying and storing now, and selling later when the price for later delivery is high enough to pay for it;
- **across quality**: blending cheaper grades into a mix that meets a buyer's specification.

### A worked example: one cargo across the Atlantic

A merchant buys a cargo of 1 million barrels of crude on the US Gulf Coast for $80.00 a barrel and finds a European refinery willing to pay $85.00 for it delivered to Rotterdam. Freight costs $3.50 a barrel, and insurance, financing and small losses in transit come to $0.40. *(The numbers are illustrative.)*

| | Per barrel | On 1 million barrels |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sale price in Rotterdam | $85.00 | $85.0 million |
| Less purchase price on the Gulf Coast | −$80.00 | −$80.0 million |
| Less freight | −$3.50 | −$3.5 million |
| Less insurance, financing and losses | −$0.40 | −$0.4 million |
| **Merchant's margin** | **$1.10** | **$1.1 million** |

The voyage takes two to three weeks. If the oil price fell by $2 while the tanker was at sea, that $1.10 would become a loss. So on the day it buys the cargo, the merchant sells futures covering the same million barrels, and buys them back when it delivers the oil. A fall in price now costs it money on the cargo and makes it the same amount on the futures, and the $1.10 survives. The merchant has turned a bet on the oil price into a business that earns a fee for logistics.

> Merchants care far more about **spreads** (the gap between two prices) than about the price itself. A merchant can make money in a falling market and lose it in a rising one, because what it trades is the difference between here and there, now and later, this grade and that.

## 4. Financial participants: capital, liquidity and risk-taking

**Banks** act as market makers, design hedges for corporate clients, finance trade and run bullion businesses. JPMorgan, HSBC and UBS are central to the London gold market, and Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley have long histories in commodity trading. In India, banks import gold as nominated agencies and offer hedging products within Reserve Bank of India rules.

**Hedge funds and proprietary trading firms** take views on prices and spreads. Systematic trend-following funds, often called **CTAs** (commodity trading advisers), buy rising markets and sell falling ones, which can amplify moves in both directions.

**Pension funds and other long-term investors** buy commodity indices to diversify and to protect against inflation. **Exchange-traded funds** give ordinary investors exposure: SPDR Gold Shares in the US and the many gold and silver ETFs in India hold real metal in vaults.

**Retail traders** are a growing force. In India, millions trade contracts on the Multi Commodity Exchange (MCX) through brokers such as Zerodha and Angel One, while UK retail investors mostly use contracts for difference (CFDs) and spread bets.

Speculators are often blamed when prices jump. But they also supply the **liquidity** that lets hedgers trade quickly and cheaply, because hedgers on opposite sides rarely turn up at the same moment or in the same size.

## 5. Infrastructure: exchanges, clearing houses, price reporters and storage

**Exchanges** host trading in standard contracts. The giants are CME Group in Chicago and New York, which owns CBOT, NYMEX and COMEX; ICE, whose London arm lists Brent crude; the London Metal Exchange, owned by Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing since 2012; China's exchanges in Shanghai, Dalian and Zhengzhou; and India's MCX, NCDEX, NSE and BSE.

**Clearing houses**, also called central counterparties, stand between every buyer and seller and guarantee that trades are honoured. That guarantee is what lets complete strangers trade safely, and Lesson 3 shows how it works.

**Price reporting agencies** such as S&P Global Platts, Argus and Fastmarkets assess prices in physical markets where no exchange exists, by surveying deals, bids and offers. Their assessments are written into a large share of the world's physical commodity contracts.

Behind all of this sits **physical infrastructure**: pipelines, tank farms such as the hub at Cushing, Oklahoma, exchange-approved metal warehouses, the gold vaults under the Bank of England, ships, and the inspectors and assayers who certify that a cargo is what it claims to be.

## 6. Governments, cartels, central banks and regulators

**OPEC**, founded in 1960, and the wider **OPEC+** group formed with Russia and others in 2016, coordinate oil output to influence prices. **Governments** hold strategic stockpiles (members of the International Energy Agency must keep emergency oil stocks equal to at least 90 days of net imports), ban exports, set import duties, fix support prices and impose sanctions. **Central banks** buy gold for their reserves and set the interest rates that shape both demand and the cost of holding inventory.

**Regulators** police the markets: the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) in the US, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) in the UK and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), with energy regulators such as FERC, Ofgem and India's CERC overseeing power and gas. Statistical agencies such as the US Energy Information Administration, the US Department of Agriculture and the India Meteorological Department publish the data that moves prices.

## Who wants what, at a glance

| Player | What they want | Typical futures position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Producer** (miner, oil company, farmer) | High, predictable selling prices | Sells futures to lock in revenue (short hedge) |
| **Consumer** (refiner, airline, food maker, jeweller) | Low, predictable input costs | Buys futures to lock in costs (long hedge) |
| **Merchant** (Vitol, Cargill and peers) | A margin on logistics, storage and blending | Both sides, hedging most price risk |
| **Bank** | Fees, spreads and financing income | Intermediary, offsetting client risk |
| **Hedge fund, CTA, retail trader** | Profit from price moves | Either side, often leveraged |
| **Index investor or ETF** | Diversification and inflation protection | Usually long, rolling contracts forward |
| **Government or central bank** | Security of supply, stable prices, reserves | Stockpiles, rules, interventions |

## Check yourself

**1. An airline wants to protect itself against a rise in jet fuel prices over the next year. Is that a long hedge or a short hedge, and why?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

A **long hedge**. The airline is a natural buyer of fuel and its risk is that prices rise, so it buys futures (or call options, or a swap) that gain value when prices rise. Those gains offset the higher price it pays for the fuel itself.

</details>

**2. Why can a commodity merchant make money in a falling market?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because it mostly earns from **spreads**, not from the price level. It hedges the price of what it holds, so a general fall in prices costs it little. What it is paid for is moving goods across space, time and quality, and those gaps can be just as wide when prices are falling.

</details>

**3. In the Atlantic example, freight jumps from $3.50 to $4.80 a barrel because war-risk insurance has soared. What happens to the trade?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

The margin becomes $85.00 − $80.00 − $4.80 − $0.40 = **−$0.20** a barrel, a loss of $200,000 on the cargo. The merchant will not do the trade at those prices. Either the European price has to rise or the Gulf Coast price has to fall until the margin is positive again, which is exactly how a jump in shipping costs pulls regional prices apart. In 2026 war-risk premiums for tankers in the Gulf rose from a fraction of a per cent of a ship's value to several per cent per transit, and trade routes shifted accordingly.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 3

- **Producers** fear falling prices and sell futures (a short hedge). **Consumers** fear rising prices and buy futures (a long hedge).
- **Merchants** move commodities across space, time and quality, hedge the price level, and care most about **spreads**.
- **Financial players** supply capital and liquidity; speculators bridge the gaps in timing and size between hedgers.
- **Infrastructure** (exchanges, clearing houses, price reporters, storage) makes trading between strangers safe, and **governments** write the rules and sometimes play.

Every participant here uses the same two layers of market: the physical one, where real barrels change hands, and the paper one, where contracts about them do. Lesson 3 takes them apart.

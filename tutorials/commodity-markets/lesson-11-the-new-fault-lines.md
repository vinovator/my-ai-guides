# Lesson 11: The New Fault Lines, 2018 to 2026

*Eleven more episodes: negative oil, Europe's gas crisis, Ukraine, nickel, lithium, cocoa, gold and silver, tariffs, rare earths, and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in 2026.*

## A decade of extremes

The years since 2018 have produced more commodity extremes than the forty before them. The first negative oil price. The highest European gas price ever recorded. A nickel price that doubled before breakfast. Record cocoa, record gold, record silver. And, in 2026, what has been described as the largest disruption to the world's oil supply in history.

Many of these episodes rerun lessons from Lesson 10 with bigger numbers: corners and squeezes, export controls, the gap between paper and physical. Others are new, and point to where commodity markets are heading: towards Asia, towards the minerals of the energy transition, and towards a world in which governments use commodities as weapons.

## 1. 2018: China launches yuan-priced crude futures

**Concepts:** where demand is, where prices are set, the dollar

In March 2018 the Shanghai International Energy Exchange launched crude oil futures priced in yuan and open to foreign traders, adding to Chinese contracts in iron ore and other materials that already reflected the world's biggest buyer.

The centre of commodity demand has moved to Asia, and price discovery is slowly following it. Debates about the "de-dollarisation" of commodity trade often start here, although the vast majority of global commodity trade is still priced in dollars, and the benchmarks in Lesson 7 still set the prices that matter.

> **The lesson:** Prices tend to be discovered where the buyers are. That process is slow, and the dollar's head start is enormous.

## 2. April 2020: Oil goes negative

**Concepts:** storage limits, the cost of carry, physical delivery, expiry

COVID lockdowns cut global oil demand by roughly a fifth at their worst, and storage tanks filled up. On **20 April 2020**, the expiring May WTI contract, which requires physical delivery at Cushing, Oklahoma, settled at **minus $37.63 a barrel**. Holders who had nowhere to put the oil paid others to take it off their hands.

The damage spread to people who had never seen Cushing. MCX's April crude contract in India, settled against the WTI price (Lesson 9), also closed below zero, and retail investors in a Chinese bank's crude oil product suffered heavy losses. OPEC+ then agreed a record output cut of 9.7 million barrels a day.

Lesson 6 explained why contango is normally capped by the cost of carry. Here storage effectively ran out, the cost of carrying a barrel became greater than the barrel itself, and the cap disappeared. Retail investors in futures-based products learned the hard way about expiry, rolling and what traders call "super contango".

> **The lesson:** Physical delivery and storage limits are real. When storage runs out, the price of a promise to deliver can fall below zero.

## 3. 2021 and 2022: Europe's energy crisis and the UK supplier collapse

**Concepts:** the merit order in power markets, unhedged positions, liquidity risk

Gas prices began climbing in 2021 as economies reopened, Asian demand for LNG rose, European storage ran low and Russian pipeline flows fell. In the UK, 29 retail energy suppliers that had sold capped tariffs to households without hedging their wholesale costs collapsed between July 2021 and May 2022, the largest of them Bulb, with around 1.5 million customers.

Then Russia invaded Ukraine. European gas, priced at the Dutch TTF hub, hit a record of about **€340 per megawatt hour** in August 2022.

The UK capped a typical household's bill at £2,500 a year through its Energy Price Guarantee, and European utilities faced margin calls so large that governments provided emergency liquidity (Lesson 4). Germany nationalised Uniper, its largest gas importer.

Three concepts are at work. In electricity markets the most expensive power station needed to meet demand sets the price for all of them (the cost curve of Lesson 5), and that station is usually gas-fired, so gas prices flowed straight into power bills. A supplier selling at a fixed price while buying at a floating one is running an unhedged bet. And liquidity risk can sink a company whose hedges are perfectly sound, because margin calls demand cash today.

> **The lesson:** Whoever sells at a fixed price and buys at a floating one is speculating, whether they know it or not. And a sound hedge can still need cash you do not have.

## 4. 2022: Russia's invasion of Ukraine

**Concepts:** sanctions, rerouted trade, price caps

Brent spiked to nearly **$140** in March 2022, and wheat hit record highs: Russia and Ukraine together had supplied about 30% of the world's wheat exports. The EU embargoed most Russian oil, and the G7 imposed a **$60 price cap** on Russian crude, enforced through Western shipping and insurance: Western firms could carry and insure Russian oil only if it was sold below the cap.

Russian oil was rerouted rather than removed, largely to India and China at a discount. Russia's share of India's crude imports rose from under 2% before the invasion to about 35% in 2024-25.

Sanctions usually redirect trade instead of eliminating supply, creating discounts, "shadow fleets" of older tankers with opaque ownership, and new trade routes. A price cap tries to cut a producer's revenue without cutting world supply. For India, discounted Russian crude became both an economic windfall and a diplomatic balancing act.

> **The lesson:** Oil finds a buyer. Sanctions change the route, the price and who profits far more than they change the total supply.

## 5. March 2022: The LME nickel squeeze

**Concepts:** squeezes in thin markets, margin spirals, the sanctity of contracts

Nickel climbed after the invasion, squeezing holders of large short positions, most famously China's Tsingshan, the world's biggest nickel producer. On 7 March the price closed near $50,000 a tonne. In the early hours of 8 March, during Asian trading, it more than doubled to above **$100,000** as holders of short positions scrambled to buy back. The London Metal Exchange suspended trading, kept it shut for eight days, and cancelled every trade made on 8 March. Two firms whose profitable trades were cancelled, the hedge fund Elliott and the trading firm Jane Street, sued the exchange and lost in the High Court in 2023. In March 2025 the FCA fined the LME £9.2 million for inadequate controls.

This was a short squeeze in a thin market, where a hedger's position was huge relative to the metal available for delivery and margin calls fed on themselves. It exposed a deep tension: exchanges must keep markets orderly, but cancelling trades undermines the certainty that contracts will be honoured. The LME introduced daily price limits afterwards.

> **The lesson:** A market is only as deep as the metal behind it. And the rule that a trade is final is worth a great deal, which is why breaking it, even to save a market, carries a lasting cost.

## 9. 2025: Tariffs split global metal markets

**Concepts:** location arbitrage, the exchange for physical, policy risk

Fear that the United States would put tariffs on imported bullion opened an unusually wide premium for gold in New York over London in early 2025. Traders flew record volumes of metal from London and Switzerland to vaults approved by COMEX (a record 151 tonnes left London's vaults in January alone), while waits to withdraw gold from the Bank of England's vaults stretched to several weeks. The gap closed only in April, when gold bars were exempted.

Copper followed in July 2025. A planned 50% US tariff on copper drove New York copper to a record premium of around 30% over the LME price. When the final order on 30 July exempted refined copper, New York prices fell about 20% in a day, the largest one-day fall on record.

Location arbitrage (Lesson 4) normally keeps one global price, but tariffs, and even the fear of them, can split it into regional prices. The gap between futures and physical metal, traded as an **exchange for physical** (EFP), became a headline indicator of stress, and policy uncertainty itself became a tradable risk.

> **The lesson:** A global price is a promise that metal can move freely. When governments threaten that freedom, the price splits along the border.

Processing chokepoints can matter more than mines. China mines about 60% of the world's rare earths, but it separates and refines about 91% of them and makes 94% of the sintered magnets, so a small market became a strategic weapon. ## 11. 2026: The Iran war and the Hormuz shock

**Concepts:** chokepoints, spare capacity, backwardation, emergency stocks, the energy-food link

Joint US and Israeli strikes on Iran began on **28 February 2026**, and within days traffic through the Strait of Hormuz collapsed. In the first half of 2025 the strait had carried about 20 million barrels a day, roughly a quarter of all oil traded by sea. On 11 March, members of the International Energy Agency agreed the largest emergency stock release in history, **400 million barrels**. QatarEnergy declared force majeure on LNG deliveries, and an Iranian missile strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex on 18 March knocked out about 17% of the country's LNG export capacity, damage expected to take three to five years to repair.

Almost every concept in this guide is at work:

- **Chokepoints matter more than reserves in the ground.** The oil was there; it could not get out.
- **The buffer was trapped.** Most of OPEC+'s spare capacity sits in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Kuwait, inside the Gulf, so the market's usual shock absorber was stuck behind the same strait, with pipelines able to carry only a fraction around it.
- **Backwardation went to extremes.** The $32 gap between physical oil and June futures in April (Lesson 6) was the market paying almost anything for barrels available now.
- **Refined products were tighter than crude.** Jet fuel and diesel rose faster than oil, which is why airlines were hit first and hardest (Lesson 8).
- **An energy shock became a food shock.** The loss of Gulf gas and fertiliser sent urea up 80% in two months, threatening next season's harvests.
- **Governments emptied their shock absorbers.** IEA stocks were released, and the US reserve fell below 300 million barrels for the first time since 1983 (Lesson 9).

The shock fell hardest on Asian importers. Japan, which in normal times receives about 93% of its crude through the strait, paid more abroad for every barrel just as its currency slid to a 40-year low, and the government spent heavily on fuel and power subsidies to cushion households. The [Japan guide](?slug=japan-economy&lesson=lesson-07-politics-war-and-a-nervous-bond-market) follows that shock through the yen, inflation and the Bank of Japan's rate decisions.

It is also a live test of the cure for high prices: emergency stocks, demand destruction, and a faster switch to renewables and electric vehicles. As of early October 2026, that test is still running.

> **The lesson:** The most important number in a supply crisis is not how much of a commodity exists, but how much can reach the people who need it, and how quickly.

## Check yourself

**1. Why could the price of WTI fall below zero in April 2020 when Brent, a cash-settled contract, did not?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because the expiring WTI contract required **physical delivery at Cushing**, where storage was almost full. Anyone still holding the contract at expiry had to take oil they had nowhere to put, so they paid others to take it. Brent futures settle in cash against an index (Lesson 3), so nobody could be forced to take barrels they could not store.

</details>

**2. A UK energy supplier sells two-year fixed-price tariffs to households and buys gas day by day in the wholesale market. Wholesale gas triples. What happens, and what should it have done?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Its revenue is fixed while its costs triple, so it loses money on every customer until it collapses, as 29 UK suppliers did in 2021 and 2022. It should have hedged: bought gas forward, or used futures or swaps, to fix its costs when it fixed its prices.

</details>

**3. In April 2026 Dated Brent was $32 a barrel above the futures price for June delivery. What does that say about stocks, and could arbitrage have closed the gap?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

It says prompt barrels were desperately scarce: extreme **backwardation**. Arbitrage could not close it, because the only way to sell oil now is to have oil now. You cannot borrow June's barrels to deliver in April, which is why backwardation has no ceiling (Lesson 6).

</details>

**4. China mines about 60% of the world's rare earths but refines about 91%. Why is the second number the one that matters most for export controls?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because ore is useless until it is separated and refined, and refining capacity takes years and specialised know-how to build. Countries that mine rare earths still send much of their ore to China to process, so controlling refining means controlling almost the whole usable supply. The chokepoint is the step that is hardest to replace.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 12

- **Storage limits** can push the price of a physically settled contract below zero.
- **Unhedged** fixed-price sellers and **margin calls** on sound hedges both turned Europe's gas crisis into a solvency crisis.
- **Sanctions reroute** trade; **squeezes** in thin markets can break even an exchange's rules.
- **Young and concentrated markets** (lithium, cocoa) run the whole cycle in a few years.
- **Gold** now trades as a monetary asset; **tariffs** can split a global price; **processing** is the new chokepoint.
- **Hormuz, 2026:** chokepoints, trapped spare capacity, extreme backwardation, emergency stocks and an energy shock turning into a food shock, all at once.

Lessons 1 to 11 explained the market. Lesson 12 asks what it means for the businesses that live inside it: banks, insurers, energy companies and manufacturers.

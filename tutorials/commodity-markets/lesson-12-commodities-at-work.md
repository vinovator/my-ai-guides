# Lesson 12: Commodities at Work

*Why commodities matter in banking, financial services and insurance, in energy, and in manufacturing: trade finance and its frauds, risk and regulation, power markets, input-cost hedging, carbon border taxes, and where technology and AI create value.*

## One cargo, many industries

Follow a single cargo of copper cathode from a mine in Chile to a cable factory in Europe, and you will meet half the financial system on the way. A bank lent the miner money against future shipments. Another bank guaranteed that the buyer would pay. Insurers covered the ship, the cargo and the political risk. A warehouse issued a receipt that a third bank accepted as collateral. The factory hedged the price on the LME and accounted for the hedge under international reporting rules. And every step burned energy that now carries a carbon price.

## Banking, financial services and insurance

### Trade and commodity finance

Banks fund the physical flow of commodities through **letters of credit**, **pre-export finance** (lending to a producer against its future shipments), **inventory and receivables finance**, and revolving facilities secured on cargoes and warehouse receipts. The oldest of these tools, the letter of credit, solves a problem as old as trade: the seller does not want to ship until it is sure of being paid, and the buyer does not want to pay until it is sure the goods have shipped.

The trick is that banks deal in **documents, not goods**. The bill of lading is a document of title: whoever holds it can claim the cargo. That makes the system efficient, and it is also its weak point. The risks are price swings in the collateral, and outright fraud. In the 2014 scandal at China's Qingdao port, the same stocks of metal were pledged to several lenders at once. In 2020 the collapse of Singapore's Hin Leong, which had hidden about $800 million of trading losses, left 23 banks owed about $3.85 billion.

### Markets businesses

Banks hedge for corporate clients, clear futures for customers, and run bullion businesses covering vaulting, lending and settlement. All of these depend on accurate positions, collateral management and pricing across physical and paper markets, which is harder than it sounds when one desk sees a cargo, another a swap and a third a futures position, all on the same underlying barrels.

### Risk and regulation

Commodity exposures feed market risk models, stress tests, counterparty credit limits and liquidity planning, and 2022 showed how quickly margin calls can drain cash (Lesson 4). Firms must report their trades under rules such as EMIR in Europe and Dodd-Frank in the US, and wholesale energy trades under REMIT in the EU and UK. They must also watch their own traders for manipulation: in 2020 JPMorgan paid $920 million to settle charges that included **spoofing** in precious metals futures, placing orders it intended to cancel in order to move prices.

### Insurance

Insurers cover crops (India's Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana is one of the world's largest crop insurance schemes), cargo and war risk at sea, and political risk. War-risk cover for Gulf shipping repriced violently in 2026, from a fraction of a per cent of a ship's value to several per cent for a single transit through the Strait of Hormuz. **Parametric** weather insurance, which pays out automatically when rainfall or temperature crosses a threshold rather than after a loss adjuster's visit, is a growing link between commodities and insurance.

### Digitisation

The UK's Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 gave electronic bills of lading the same legal standing as paper ones, and India's electronic negotiable warehouse receipts let farmers and traders borrow against stored produce. Both reduce the paper trail that fraudsters exploit: a digital title registered once is much harder to pledge twice.

## Energy

Energy companies sit on every side of the market. Producers sell oil and gas, refiners process it, traders move it, and utilities generate power and sell it to households. They manage all of this through **energy trading and risk management** (ETRM) systems that capture trades, value positions daily, track credit exposure and schedule physical deliveries.

**Power markets are becoming the most complex commodity market of all.** Electricity cannot be stored cheaply in bulk, so supply must match demand every second, and the price is set by the most expensive power station needed at that moment: the cost curve of Lesson 5, applied to generators, which the industry calls the **merit order**.

More wind and solar means more volatile prices. On a sunny, windy afternoon, plants with near-zero running costs can meet all demand and prices can fall below zero; on a still winter evening, gas plants set the price and it soars. That volatility creates demand for batteries, for flexible demand that shifts use to cheap hours, and for sophisticated weather forecasting. Corporate buyers sign long-term **power purchase agreements** to fix their costs, and India's electricity futures, launched in July 2025, give its utilities their first exchange-traded hedge. Wholesale energy markets carry their own integrity rules: REMIT in the EU and UK, FERC oversight in the US and CERC in India.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturers are commodity consumers whose margins depend on input costs: steel, aluminium and copper for engineering and cars, oil and gas derivatives for plastics and chemicals, energy for everything, and crops for food and drink.

| Tool | How it works | What it protects against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Index-linked supply contracts** | Price set as a benchmark plus a premium, for example the LME price plus a delivery premium | Overpaying relative to the market |
| **Cost pass-through clauses** | Customer prices adjust automatically with input costs | Margin squeeze when costs rise |
| **Futures and swap hedges** | Fix the price of future purchases | Price spikes |
| **Safety stocks** | Hold extra inventory | Physical shortages and delays |
| **Dual sourcing** | Buy from more than one supplier or country | A single point of failure |

Accounting matters as much as trading. **Hedge accounting** rules (IFRS 9 internationally, ASC 815 in the US) let companies show hedges alongside the exposures they protect, so that a sensible hedge does not make earnings look volatile every quarter.

Two newer pressures stand out. **Critical-mineral export controls** showed in 2025 that a shortage of rare earth magnets can halt a car plant (Lesson 11). And **carbon now carries a price at the border.** The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered its definitive phase in January 2026, charging for the carbon embedded in imported iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity, so that imports pay a carbon price comparable to what EU producers pay.

The charge phases in slowly, matching the phase-out of the free carbon allowances EU producers receive: 2.5% of the full charge applies to 2026 imports, rising to 100% by 2034, and the certificates for 2026 imports go on sale in February 2027. CBAM matters greatly for Indian exporters of steel and aluminium, and the UK plans its own version from January 2027.

## Where technology and AI create value

Every section above contains a data problem. That is where technology, and increasingly AI, earns its keep.

| Business problem | What firms need | Where technology and AI help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Price risk visibility** | One live view of physical and paper exposure | Data platforms, integrated trading and risk systems (ETRM and CTRM), risk analytics |
| **Forecasting** | Better views of supply, demand, weather and prices | Machine learning, satellite imagery, ship tracking, alternative data |
| **Liquidity and margin** | Cash needs under stress scenarios | Treasury analytics, collateral optimisation, scenario engines |
| **Compliance and surveillance** | Detect spoofing and manipulation; automate reporting | AI-assisted trade surveillance, automated regulatory reporting |
| **Trade operations and fraud** | Faster, safer documents and collateral checks | Document AI, electronic trade documents, digital warehouse receipts |
| **Procurement** | Fair pricing and resilient sourcing | Should-cost models, supplier risk analytics, contract analysis |
| **Carbon** | Accurate emissions data for trading schemes and border taxes | Carbon accounting and product-footprint platforms |

The pattern across the table is that the hard part is rarely the model. It is joining up data that lives in different places (a cargo in an operations system, a hedge in a trading system, a margin call in treasury, an emissions figure in a sustainability report) so that a single question, "what happens to us if oil rises $30?", can be answered in minutes rather than weeks. In a margin-call crisis like 2022's, a firm that can answer it quickly has time to arrange credit; one that cannot finds out from the clearing house.

## Check yourself

**1. Why would a copper exporter in Chile prefer a letter of credit from the buyer's bank to the buyer's own promise to pay?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Because it swaps the buyer's credit risk for a bank's. Under a letter of credit, the bank promises to pay as soon as the exporter presents documents that comply with its terms, typically the bill of lading and inspection certificates, whatever happens to the buyer's finances or mood afterwards. The exporter can ship knowing it will be paid.

</details>

**2. How could the same stock of metal be used to borrow from three banks, and what makes that harder today?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

By issuing or forging more than one warehouse receipt for the same metal, and pledging each receipt to a different bank that never checks with the others, which is what happened at Qingdao in 2014. Electronic receipts and bills of lading registered once in a recognised system, and independent inspection of the stock itself, make double-pledging much harder.

</details>

**3. An EU importer brings in 5,000 tonnes of aluminium whose production emitted 8 tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne. With a carbon price of €80 and no carbon price paid where it was made, what is the full CBAM charge, and what share applies in 2026?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Embedded emissions: 5,000 × 8 = 40,000 tonnes. Full charge: 40,000 × €80 = **€3.2 million**, or €640 a tonne of aluminium. In 2026 only 2.5% applies, about **€80,000**, rising each year to the full amount by 2034.

</details>

## What to carry into Lesson 13

- **Banks** finance the physical flow (letters of credit, pre-export and inventory finance) and deal in documents, which is both the system's efficiency and its weak point for **fraud**.
- **Risk and regulation** run on positions, margin and reporting; **insurers** price crop, cargo, war and political risk, increasingly with parametric cover.
- **Power markets** set prices at the marginal plant, so more renewables mean more volatility and occasional negative prices.
- **Manufacturers** manage input costs with index-linked contracts, pass-through clauses, hedges, safety stocks and dual sourcing, and now face **carbon at the border**.
- Technology creates value mostly by **joining up data**: the same exposure seen in one place, in time to act.

Lesson 13 turns everything in the guide into a set of habits for reading the market yourself.

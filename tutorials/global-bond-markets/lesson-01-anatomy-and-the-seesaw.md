# Lesson 1: Anatomy and the Seesaw

*What a bond is, the six words you need, and the one mechanism everything else rests on.*

---

## What a bond actually is

A bond is a loan turned into a tradable piece of paper.

That sentence is worth pausing on, because it contains the whole subject. When you buy a bond you are not buying a share of something. You are **lending**. A government or a company takes your money, promises to pay you interest at fixed intervals, and promises to return the original sum on a set date. What makes it a bond rather than a private loan is the second half: the promise is written in a standard form, so you can sell it to somebody else without asking the borrower's permission.

The bond market is where these loans are created and then traded. It matters for a reason that has nothing to do with wanting to own one: the interest rates it produces become the price of money for almost everybody else. And it is enormous. Bonds outstanding worldwide were worth about **$160.7 trillion** in 2025, slightly more than the **$157.8 trillion** value of all the world's listed shares.

## The anatomy of a bond

Imagine the UK government wants to borrow £100 from you for ten years. It gives you a **gilt**, the name for a UK government bond. The gilt promises you £5 a year, and your £100 back at the end.

That single example contains almost all the vocabulary.

Four terms describe the contract itself, and they never change once the bond is issued:

| Term | In this example | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Issuer** | The UK government | The borrower who created the loan |
| **Face value** (or par) | £100 | The sum repaid in full at the end |
| **Coupon** | 5%, or £5 a year | The interest rate, fixed at issue, as a percentage of face value |
| **Maturity** | Ten years from now | The date the loan ends and the face value is repaid |

The word *coupon* is a fossil. Bond certificates used to be printed with detachable paper tabs, and holders physically clipped one off and presented it to claim each interest payment.

### The two terms where beginners stumble

The other two terms describe not the contract but the *market*, and they are where confusion usually begins.

- The **price** is what the bond sells for today. It can be above or below £100, and it changes continuously.
- The **yield** is the total annual return you would earn by buying at today's price and holding to maturity. It counts both the coupons *and* any gain or loss between what you paid and the £100 you eventually get back.

Moves in yields are measured in **basis points**, each one a hundredth of a percentage point. A rise of 25 basis points takes a yield from 5.00% to 5.25%. The abbreviation is *bp* or *bps*, and you will hear traders say "twenty-five beeps."

> Coupon and yield are different things, and conflating them is the most common beginner error. The coupon is a fixed promise printed on the bond. The yield is what the market decides that promise is currently worth.

---

## The seesaw: why prices and yields move in opposite directions

This is the single most important idea in the subject, so it is worth taking slowly.

Suppose you pay £100 for that gilt, locking in £5 a year. Shortly afterwards, interest rates rise, and the government begins selling brand-new ten-year gilts paying **£6** a year for the same £100.

Now you want to sell. Nobody will pay you £100 for a bond yielding £5 a year when an identical-risk bond yielding £6 is available at the same price. Your bond is not worthless, but it is worth *less*. You have to drop your price.

How far? Far enough that a buyer purchasing at your new price earns the going rate of 6% overall. Their return comes from two sources: the £5 a year they collect, plus the bonus of paying less than £100 today and still receiving the full £100 at maturity. Those two together have to add up to 6% a year. That happens at a price of about **£92.60**.

The story runs in reverse too. If new gilts paid only 4%, your £5 a year would be the attractive one, and buyers would compete for it until the price reached about **£108**.

> An existing bond's coupon never changes. Its **price** keeps adjusting until its **yield** matches whatever the market currently demands. That is why "yields rose" and "bond prices fell" are the same headline written two ways.

### Why the coupon cannot simply be adjusted

It sometimes helps to ask the obvious question: why does the price have to move at all? Why not just pay the holder more?

Because the coupon is a contractual promise, printed on the instrument and identical for every holder of that bond. The issuer cannot pay one holder £5 and another £6. The only variable left that can respond to changing conditions is what the bond *trades for*. The price is the market's only free parameter, so the price does all the work.

## How far prices move: the effect of maturity

Not all bonds react equally. Here is the same 5% bond at three different maturities, priced at a range of market yields. (Simplified to one coupon payment a year; real gilts pay twice a year, which changes the figures slightly but not the pattern.)

| Market yield | 2-year | 10-year | 30-year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3% | £103.83 | £117.06 | £139.20 |
| 4% | £101.89 | £108.11 | £117.29 |
| **5%** | **£100.00** | **£100.00** | **£100.00** |
| 6% | £98.17 | £92.64 | £86.24 |
| 7% | £96.38 | £85.95 | £75.18 |

Three lessons come out of that table.

**First, all three bonds are worth exactly £100 when the market yield equals their 5% coupon.** This is the anchor. A bond trades at par when the market wants precisely what the bond already pays. Above par is called trading at a *premium*, below par at a *discount*.

**Second, longer bonds swing far more.** If yields rise from 5% to 6%, the 2-year loses under 2%, the 10-year about 7%, and the 30-year about 14%. The reason is intuitive once stated: the owner of a long bond is stuck with a below-market coupon for many more years. Two years of being underpaid by £1 is a minor annoyance. Thirty years of it is a serious loss, and the price has to fall far enough today to compensate a buyer for all of it.

**Third, the moves are lopsided in the holder's favour**, which we come to shortly.

---

## Duration: the number that predicts the swing

That second lesson has a name and a number. **Duration** measures how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in yields.

The practical version is a rule of thumb worth memorising:

> Duration tells you roughly what percentage a bond's price moves for each one-percentage-point change in yield: in the opposite direction.

For the three bonds above:

| Bond | Duration | Predicted move if yields rise 1 point | Actual move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2-year, 5% coupon | 1.9 | −1.9% | −1.83% |
| 10-year, 5% coupon | 7.7 | −7.7% | −7.36% |
| 30-year, 5% coupon | 15.4 | −15.4% | −13.76% |

The predictions are close, and they get less accurate the longer the bond: for a reason we are about to meet.

### Why the 10-year's duration is 7.7, not 10

This is the detail that makes duration click, and most introductions skip it.

You might expect a ten-year bond to have a duration of ten. It does not, and the reason is that you do not wait ten years for all of your money. You get £5 back after one year, another £5 after two, and so on. Only the final £105 arrives at year ten.

Duration is the *average* time you wait for your money, weighting each payment by how much it is worth today. Those early coupons pull the average down below ten. So:

- **A bond's duration is always less than its maturity**, unless it pays no coupons at all.
- **The bigger the coupon, the shorter the duration**, because more of your money comes back early.
- **A zero-coupon bond has a duration exactly equal to its maturity**, because there is only one payment and it arrives at the end.

That last point explains why the most violent bonds in existence are long-dated and low-coupon. Lesson 6 has a bond maturing in 2120 with a 0.85% coupon, whose price fell roughly 75% in three years.

> **Duration is not maturity.** Two thirty-year bonds can behave completely differently if one pays a 7% coupon and the other pays 1%. When a fund or a bank reports its interest-rate exposure, duration is the number it reports, not average maturity.

## Convexity: the asymmetry in your favour

Look once more at the 30-year row. A one-point *fall* in yields, from 5% to 4%, gains you £17.29. A one-point *rise*, from 5% to 6%, costs you £13.76.

Same size move, different size outcome, and the difference favours the holder. Gains are bigger than losses. This lopsidedness is called **convexity**, and it is a property of the arithmetic rather than a market opinion.

The picture makes it obvious. Duration is a straight line, a local approximation. The true price-yield relationship is a curve, and the curve always sits *above* the straight line.

Read the green marks. At 3%, duration predicts a price of £130.74 but the bond is actually worth £139.20, so you gained **more** than predicted. At 7%, duration predicts £69.26 but the bond is actually worth £75.18, so you lost **less** than predicted. The curve bends away from the line in the holder's favour in both directions.

Convexity is why duration is only a local approximation, and why the approximation degrades for big moves and long bonds. It is also genuinely valuable: for two bonds with the same duration, the more convex one is worth more, and investors pay for it.

## The insight that rescues everything: holding to maturity

All of the above describes what happens to a bond's *market price*. It is easy to read it and conclude that bonds are dangerous. That conclusion needs one crucial qualification.

If you hold a bond to maturity and the issuer pays in full, **these price swings wash out entirely**. You collect every coupon and you get your £100 back. The fact that the bond was briefly worth £86 in year four is a historical curiosity; it changed nothing about what you received.

You have still given something up. You may have missed out on higher rates available elsewhere, that is a real opportunity cost. Inflation may have shrunk what that £100 buys, which Lesson 2 takes seriously. But you did not lose pounds.

Price risk bites in exactly two circumstances:

1. **You have to sell early.** Then you crystallise whatever the market will pay today.
2. **You must value your holdings at today's prices.** Banks, insurers and funds are required to do this. A loss that exists only on paper can still breach a capital requirement, frighten a depositor, or trigger a margin call.

That second circumstance is not a technicality. It is precisely what destroyed a large American bank in 2023, and Lesson 6 tells the story.

## What to carry into Lesson 2

- A bond is a tradable loan. Issuer, face value, coupon and maturity are fixed at issue; price and yield are set by the market and move continuously.
- Price and yield move in **opposite directions**, because the coupon cannot change so the price must.
- **Duration** measures the sensitivity and is always less than maturity for a coupon-paying bond. Long, low-coupon bonds are the most volatile things in the market.
- **Convexity** means gains slightly exceed losses for equal moves in yield.
- Hold to maturity and the swings are irrelevant. Sell early, or mark to market, and they are everything.

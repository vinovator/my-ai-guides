# Lesson 4: The Gravitational Pull

*Why government bond yields set the price of almost everything else, and why that gives bond investors power over governments.*

---

## The base price of money

Here is the reason to care about bonds even if you never intend to own one.

A government bond yield is the return available for taking essentially no default risk in that currency. That makes it a floor. No rational lender accepts less than the risk-free rate to take on *more* risk, so every other interest rate in the economy is built on top of it: the government yield, plus a spread for whatever additional risk is involved.

Move the floor and everything above it moves. This is why bond yields are sometimes called the **base price of money**, and why a change in them ripples outward into places that look unconnected.

## Where the ripples land

### They set borrowing costs

The most direct effect, and the one most people feel personally, is on mortgages. But the transmission mechanism differs sharply by country, and the difference is instructive.

| Country | How a home loan is priced | What the borrower actually tracks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **United States** | The popular 30-year fixed mortgage rate tracks the **10-year Treasury yield** plus a margin | The bond market |
| **United Kingdom** | Fixed-rate mortgages are priced off **swap rates**, which move closely with gilt yields. Lenders often reprice within days of a gilt sell-off | The bond market, with a short lag |
| **India** | Most floating-rate home loans made since 2019 are linked **directly to the RBI's repo rate** | The central bank |

That last row is genuinely different in kind. Indian borrowers feel monetary policy decisions rather than bond market moves, because the link is contractual rather than market-mediated. An Indian homeowner watches the RBI's meeting calendar; an American homeowner watches the 10-year.

Company borrowing follows the same benchmarks. A firm issuing a ten-year bond pays the ten-year government yield plus its credit spread, so a general rise in yields raises its costs even if nothing about the firm has changed.

### They squeeze or relieve government budgets

Every government rolls over maturing debt and issues new debt continuously. When yields rise, each new tranche costs more, and the interest line in the budget grows.

This is money that cannot be spent on anything else. A large enough rise in yields can force a government to choose between cutting services, raising taxes, or borrowing still more, which may push yields higher again.

That dynamic gives bond investors real leverage over elected governments, and it has a name: **bond vigilantes**. The term describes investors who sell a government's debt to punish policies they judge reckless. It sounds like a conspiracy and is nothing of the sort; it is simply what happens when enough independent buyers reach the same conclusion and step back at once. Lesson 6 has two episodes where the pressure changed government policy within days, one of them ending two political careers.

### They set the value of almost everything else

This is the least obvious ripple and arguably the most powerful.

Any asset expected to produce cash in future is worth, today, that future cash discounted back to the present. The rate used for discounting is built on the risk-free rate. When the risk-free rate rises, the same future cash is worth less today, so the asset's value falls, **even if nothing about the asset has changed**.

Two consequences follow:

- **Shares tend to fall when yields rise.** The mechanism is arithmetic, not sentiment.
- **Fast-growing companies are hit hardest.** If most of a company's expected profits lie far in the future, those distant profits are discounted most heavily. This is why rising yields punish speculative technology shares more than established, cash-generating ones.

Property is revalued by exactly the same logic, which is why commercial real estate suffers when yields rise even while rents are unchanged.

### They move currencies, pensions and banks

**Currencies.** Higher yields usually attract foreign money, because international investors chase return, and that demand lifts the currency. But there is a crucial exception. When yields rise because investors are *losing faith* in a government, the currency and the bonds fall **together**. That combination is one of the most dangerous signals in finance, and Lesson 8 makes it a diagnostic question in its own right.

**Pensions.** Annuity rates track long-term gilt yields, so the income a retiree can buy with a pension pot moves with the bond market. Defined-benefit pension schemes are affected even more directly, because the value of what they owe is itself calculated by discounting at a bond-derived rate.

**Banks.** Banks hold large bond portfolios, which gain and lose value with rates. Lesson 1 noted that price swings wash out if you hold to maturity, but banks may be required to value holdings at market prices, and depositors can demand their money at any time. The combination is how an unrealised loss becomes a real one.

## The bond market as a forecast

There is one more function, and it is useful even if you never trade.

Bond investors are pricing inflation and growth years ahead, with their own money, continuously. The result is one of the best real-time readings available of what professional money actually expects, not what commentators say, but what large sums are positioned for.

Several specific readings fall out of this:

- **The yield curve's shape** reveals the expected path of central bank rates.
- **The breakeven rate** (Lesson 2) reveals expected inflation.
- **Credit spreads** reveal expected corporate distress.
- **The gap between two countries' yields** reveals expected currency and policy divergence.

None of these is a prophecy. Markets are frequently wrong, sometimes for years. But they are a better-informed guess than most alternatives, and they update continuously.

## Three episodes, in one line each

To make the power concrete before Lesson 6 examines them properly:

- **UK, September 2022.** A budget of large unfunded tax cuts sent gilt yields soaring. Pension funds using borrowed money were forced to sell into the fall, pushing yields higher still. The Bank of England made emergency purchases. Within weeks the Chancellor was sacked and the Prime Minister resigned.
- **US, March 2023.** Silicon Valley Bank had invested deposits in long-dated bonds bought when yields were very low. As the Fed raised rates, those safe bonds lost value. When depositors withdrew, the bank had to sell at a loss, triggering a run and its collapse.
- **US, April 2025.** A sharp Treasury sell-off followed sweeping tariff announcements. It was widely seen as a key reason the White House paused the steepest tariffs a week later, and the President said he had been watching the bond market.

Notice what those three have in common. In each case, the bond market did not merely *reflect* events. It **forced** them.

## What to carry into Lesson 5

- The government yield is the **floor** under every other interest rate, so moving it moves everything above it.
- It sets **borrowing costs**, though India transmits through the policy rate rather than the bond market.
- It **squeezes budgets**, which is the source of bond investors' political leverage.
- It **re-values every other asset** by changing the rate at which future cash is discounted, hitting long-dated growth assets hardest.
- Rising yields normally **lift a currency**, unless they are rising because confidence is draining, in which case both fall together.

Lesson 5 takes the machine into three real markets and then watches all of it happen at once.
